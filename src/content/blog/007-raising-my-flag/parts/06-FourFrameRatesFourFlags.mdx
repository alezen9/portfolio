## Four frame rates, four flags

This is my favourite finding of the whole project, and the one I would have never spotted by just looking at the flag.

I didn't want to guess how the July version behaved, so I ported its compute kernels line by line to a small Node script. A CPU
replica of the simulation, basically, where I could run the flag at any frame rate I wanted and measure things like how much each
edge stretched and where the corners ended up. The only difference was the wind noise, frozen at its middle value so every run was
repeatable.

Then I ran the calm flag, no wind event, at four different frame rates:

| Frame rate | Average stretch | Worst edge | Bottom corner height |
| ---------- | --------------- | ---------- | -------------------- |
| 30 Hz      | 14.7%           | 33.8%      | -2.18 m              |
| 60 Hz      | 5.8%            | 30.3%      | -1.09 m              |
| 120 Hz     | 2.2%            | 25.7%      | -0.40 m              |
| 144 Hz     | 1.8%            | 25.8%      | -0.28 m              |

Same code, same flag, a different shape at every frame rate. At 30 Hz it stretched seven times more and hung 1.8 metres lower than at
120 Hz, deep into the grass. On my 120 Hz laptop it looked fine, which is exactly why I never noticed.

<br />

### Why

The cause was hiding in plain sight in Jakobsen's paper. Right after introducing Verlet he writes "keeping the time step fixed", and I
wasn't.

Verlet stores the velocity as the distance travelled in the last step. If the next step is longer or shorter, that same distance now
means a different speed, and the cloth gains or loses energy. On top of that, the stiffness of position based constraints depends on
how many relaxation passes run per second, and with a variable frame rate that number changes too. Even the speed cap was per step,
so it allowed 4.5 m/s at 30 Hz and 18 m/s at 120.

<br />

### And it stretched

The table has a second problem in it. Real cloth stretches 0 to 5%, and my worst edges, the ones right next to the pole, stretched
between 25 and 34%. In a wind event the whole flag sat at about 10% of stretch, which is exactly the slack I had given the tethers. In
other words, in wind it was the tether holding the flag's shape, not the cloth.

Gravity was also set to 8 instead of 9.81. My best guess is that I lowered it at some point because the flag looked too stretched,
which in hindsight was treating the symptom :)

<br />

The fix for the frame rate part is well known and it's what the Three.js cloth example and NvCloth both do: a **fixed time step**.
Accumulate the time that passed, run as many fixed steps as fit into it, keep the remainder for the next frame. With that change alone,
the replica produced identical results at 30, 60, 120 and 144 Hz.

Fixing the stretch needed something else.
