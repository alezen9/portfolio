## Chapter III: Beyond Ripples

Months passed before I came back to the lake again.

The spark this time wasn't my own, it came from a [post on X](https://x.com/psergiomr/status/1899840041288917427) which
completely stunned me. It came from a post on X that completely stunned me. The water in it shimmered and breathed in
a way I hadn't even imagined for Revo. I bookmarked it, walked away, and told myself: _one day_.

When I did return, I'd just spent some time tinkering in Godot, playing with stylized water shaders and soaking up
whatever knowledge I could find. But Revo was still my main playground, so I carried what I'd learned back into
Three.js and kept building there.

I wanted to capture the mood of that post, but make it my own.

What followed wasn't one breakthrough, but a stack of small pieces, each filling in part of the picture: normals to
shape the ripples, depth to give the water body, refraction to bend the world beneath, reflections and Fresnel to tie
the surface to the sky, highlights and glints to make it sparkle, Beer-Lambert absorption for color and weight, and a
simple opacity trick to balance clarity up close with solidity at a distance.

Each one could be its own story. Together they layered into something that finally felt like the lake I'd pictured
from the start.

<br />

### Surface Normals & Waves

The surface is always the starting point. No matter what tricks come later: depth falloff, absorption, reflections, if
the ripples don't feel alive, the illusion collapses.

This version reused the normal maps from Chapter II, but with a twist. I sampled the same water normal twice, at
different scales and scroll directions, then blended them using RNM, which preserves directionality and detail
instead of giving the mushy look of naive interpolation.

<br />

```typescript
class WaterMaterial extends MeshBasicNodeMaterial {
  // ...
  private sampleNormal = Fn(([uv = vec2(0)]) => {
    const tex = texture(waterNormalTexture, uv);
    return tex.mul(2).sub(1).rgb.normalize();
  });

  private createMaterial() {
    // ...
    const nUV1 = uv().add(frequency).mul(uUvScale.mul(1.37)).fract();
    const tsn1 = this.sampleNormal(nUV1);
    const nUV2 = uv().sub(frequency).mul(uUvScale.mul(0.73)).fract();
    const tsn2 = this.sampleNormal(nUV2);
    const blendedTsn = utils.blendRNM(tsn1, tsn2);
    const tsn = vec3(blendedTsn.xy.mul(uNormalScale), blendedTsn.z).normalize();
    const normal = tsn.x
      .mul(uTworld)
      .add(tsn.y.mul(uBworld))
      .add(tsn.z.mul(uNworld))
      .normalize();
    // ...
  }
}
```

The odd multipliers (`1.37` and `0.73`) aren't random. If I'd picked clean ratios like `0.5` and `1.25`, the scrolls
would eventually sync up and reveal a repeating pattern. Using awkward, almost-irrational numbers keeps the two layers
drifting forever without tiling in lockstep, a dirt-cheap way to break repetition.

Another small but important detail: I only scale the X and Y components of the tangent-space normal (`tsn`).
Those represent sideways tilt, the part that makes ripples catch the light. Scaling Z (the "up" axis) would just squash
the surface flat and kill the contrast. It's a subtle distinction, but it makes a big difference in how natural the
water feels.

And finally, blending itself. In my earlier attempt I just averaged two normals with `mix(n1, n2, 0.5)`. It looked fine
at a glance, but it washed out the detail and left the surface feeling flat. RNM (Reoriented Normal Mapping) avoids
that by reorienting one normal into the other's space before combining, so the fine detail survives. If you're curious,
Self Shadow's [article](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/blending-in-detail/) is the gold standard breakdown of
RNM, UDN, and other variants.

<br />

### Depth

Normals gave the surface life, but the lake still felt hollow, like ripples painted on glass.

What I needed was depth. Not in the everyday sense of how far something sinks under water, but in 3D graphics terms:
**how far each fragment is from the camera**.

In Three.js TSL, the entry point is `viewportDepthTexture(uv)`. That gives you the value from the depth buffer, but it
isn't in world units, it's in clip space. After projection, X, Y, and Z all get squeezed into a normalized cube (NDC,
or Normalized Device Coordinates).

Here's where it gets tricky: different APIs disagree on the Z range. WebGL/OpenGL use `[-1..1]`, while WebGPU (along
with DirectX and Metal) use `[0..1]`. A tiny detail, but if you ignore it, the math falls apart.

I learned that the hard way: everything looked perfect in Chrome with WebGPU... until I opened Firefox (WebGL2) and
my lake collapsed into nonsense.

<br />

**From clip space to linear depth**

The goal here is "linear" depth: the actual camera-space distance.

The usual way to recover linear depth is to take the fragment's position in clip space and multiply it by the
inverse projection matrix. That brings it back into view space, and from there you can grab the Z value as the true
distance.

It works, but it felt like overkill, a full 4&times;4 matrix multiplication per fragment, just to get one number.

While digging around, I came across [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NCXr8zrT5zs) excellent video that showed a
cleaner path. Since I only needed the Z, the math collapses: we can cherry-pick just two elements of the projection
matrix (I called them p3z and p2z) and compute it with a single division:

<br />

```math
z_{linear} = \frac{p_{3z}}{z_{ndc} + p_{2z}}
```

<br />

That shortcut spared me from pushing an entire inverse matrix into the shader and suddenly made depth cheap enough
to use everywhere.

Here's what it looks like in my shader:

```typescript
//...
const zNdc = viewportDepthTexture(screenUV).r;
const isWebGL = float(1).sub(uIsWebGPU);
const webglZNdc = zNdc.mul(2).sub(1).mul(isWebGL);
const webgpuZNdc = zNdc.mul(uIsWebGPU);
const zNdcCompatible = webglZNdc.add(webgpuZNdc);
const p3z = cameraProjectionMatrix.element(3).element(2);
const p2z = cameraProjectionMatrix.element(2).element(2);
const zLinear = p3z.div(zNdcCompatible.add(p2z));
const fragLinear = positionView.z.negate();
const isUnderWater = step(fragLinear, zLinear);
const fragmentDepth = zLinear.sub(fragLinear);
const waterDepth = fragmentDepth.div(uDepthDistance).clamp();
//...
```

Three.js doesn't expose a reliable flag for WebGPU vs WebGL, even though it adapts internally, so I hooked up my own.
By checking `navigator.gpu?.requestAdapter()`, I set a uniform `uIsWebGPU`, and used that to drive whether I treat
depth as `[0..1]` or `[-1..1]`.

Branching (`if/else`) would also work, but GPUs are happiest when every thread follows the same path; even a tiny
conditional can slow things down. Driving it with a uniform avoids that cost.

Another important detail: Three.js matrices are column-major (like GLSL). If you're cross-checking OpenGL docs, which
often show row-major, the indices appear shifted. For example, what looks like row 3, col 2 in the docs is actually
`.element(2).element(3)` in Three.js. That mismatch tripped me until I realized it was just layout, not math.

If you want to dive deeper into how projection matrices shape this math, [WebGPU fundamentals — perspective projection](https://webgpufundamentals.org/webgpu/lessons/webgpu-perspective-projection.html)
and [Song Ho's OpenGL projection matrix breakdown](https://www.songho.ca/opengl/gl_projectionmatrix.html) helped me a lot.

<br />

Depth was one of those invisible victories. At this point, I wasn't even using it for anything fancy yet, but I knew
it was going to be a cornerstone for multiple effects. It drives how strong refraction bends the background, how quickly
light is absorbed with distance, even how the surface should fade near the shoreline. Without depth, all of
those are blind guesses.

<br />

### Refraction

With depth in place, I could finally put it to work.
The first effect I tackled was refraction, the way water bends the world behind it.

Look into a shallow lake and the pebbles below look sharp. Step deeper and everything starts to wobble and drift, as
if the world itself bends. That's refraction: light changing direction when it crosses from air into water.

In the shader, I faked it by offsetting the screen texture with ripple normals. The trick is simple: take the
tangent-space normal's X/Y tilt (not Z, that's "up", which doesn't bend UVs), scale it by depth, and use it to
distort the screen UVs.

In TSL, `viewportTexture(uv)` is basically a live snapshot of the scene already rendered to the screen. Feed it
`screenUV` (ranging 0-1 across the viewport) and it hands you back the color at that pixel. Refraction is just
re-sampling that snapshot at slightly shifted UVs, so the background bends with the ripples.

```typescript
//...
// min and max refraction strength here are arbitrary
const distortionStrength = mix(
  uRefractionStrength,
  uRefractionStrength.mul(1.5),
  waterDepth,
);

const distortion = tsn.xy.mul(distortionStrength); // tangent tilt drives wobble
const refractedScreenUv = screenUV.add(distortion.mul(isUnderWater));
//...
```

This gives the wobble, but it isn't enough. Distortion can push UVs behind occluding geometry—pulling colors from
places that shouldn't be visible. Without a fix, you end up with stretched pixels or peeking through geometry.

The fix is to re-sample depth at the distorted UV, and check if it's actually valid:

```typescript
//...
const zNdcRefr = viewportDepthTexture(refractedScreenUv).r;
const webglZNdcRefr = zNdcRefr.mul(2).sub(1).mul(isWebGL);
const webgpuZNdcRefr = zNdcRefr.mul(uIsWebGPU);
const zNdcCompatibleRefr = webglZNdcRefr.add(webgpuZNdcRefr);
const zLinearRefr = p3z.div(zNdcCompatibleRefr.add(p2z));
const isSafe = step(fragLinear, zLinearRefr);
const fragmentDepthRefr = zLinearRefr.sub(fragLinear);
const waterDepthRefr = fragmentDepthRefr.div(uDepthDistance).clamp();
const safeScreenUv = mix(screenUV, refractedScreenUv, isSafe);
const screenColor = viewportTexture(safeScreenUv).rgb;
//...
```

The `isSafe` mask ensures I only pull colors from fragments that actually exist in front of the camera. If the distorted
UV points behind geometry, I fall back to the original `screenUV`. A tiny guardrail, but it saves the effect from falling
apart.

Refraction also scales with depth. In shallow spots, distortion stays subtle; deeper down, I let it grow stronger.
Without that, shallow water would wobble just as much as deep water, which feels wrong.

It was the first moment my lake stopped being a surface effect and started interacting with the world around it.
The background bent and rippled through the water, subtle, imperfect, but finally grounded in the scene.

Of course, water doesn't just bend the world; sometimes it mirrors it. That's where reflections, and Fresnel, come in.

<br />

### Reflections, Fresnel & Viewing Angle

Refraction made the lake feel connected to the world beneath.
The next step was tying the surface to the sky and its surroundings.

I kept it simple: an environment cubemap blended using a Fresnel weight.

<br />

**Reflections**

I built a view direction from the camera to each fragment, reflected it around the surface normal, and sampled
the cubemap:

```typescript
const viewDir = normalize(cameraPosition.sub(positionWorld));
const reflectVector = reflect(viewDir.negate(), normal);
const reflectedColor = cubeTexture(cubeMapTexture, reflectVector);
```

This gave a convincing mirror-like color, but applied uniformly, it felt wrong. Water shouldn't be equally reflective
when you look straight down and when you glance across the surface.

<br />

**Fresnel (view-angle weighting)**

That's where Fresnel comes in. Dielectrics like water have a small head-on reflectance that ramps up toward the horizon.
I used Schlick's approximation:

<br />

```math
F(\theta) = F_0 + (1 - F_0) (1 - \cos\theta)^5
```

<br />

Where:

- $$F_0$$ is the reflectivity at head-on, computed as $$F_0\approx((n-1)/(n+1))^2$$. For water it's around 0.02 (~2%).
- $$\cos\theta$$ is the dot between the surface normal and the view direction: the viewing angle.
- The exponent `5` isn't arbitrary, it's Schlick's trick, a balance between accuracy and efficiency. In reality,
  Fresnel is derived from more complex equations involving polarization and material IOR, but this approximated form gets
  close enough while being dirt cheap for real-time graphics.

<br />

In code:

```typescript
const cosTheta = dot(normal, viewDir).clamp();
const F0 = float(0.02);
const g = float(1.0).sub(cosTheta); // grazing term
const g5 = g.mul(g).mul(g).mul(g).mul(g); // (1 - cosθ)^5
const fresnelSchlick = F0.add(float(1).sub(F0).mul(g5));
const fresnelWeight = fresnelSchlick.mul(uFresnelScale).clamp();
```

<br />

With that, reflections fade in at grazing angles and fade out when looking down, where refraction and absorption
take over. It's a small detail, but the kind your eye expects. Suddenly, the lake stops feeling like a static sticker
and starts behaving like a real surface.

> One caveat: a cubemap has no parallax, so nearby objects don't slide correctly in reflection.
> It's great for “a sense of sky” and distant scenery. Screen-space reflections (SSR) would be the next logical
> step, but that's a story for another time.

This Fresnel-weighted reflection became one of the two main colors I blend at the end (the other is the "through-water"
color from absorption).

But water isn't just a mirror, it eats light. Before I added the shiny bits, I needed the color to sink with thickness.
There goes Beer-Lambert.

<br />

### Beer-Lambert: Depth-Based Absorption

Refraction bends the background; absorption decides how much of it survives the trip through water.

If you've ever looked into a lake, you know the water isn't truly transparent. Shallow parts look almost clear, but as
depth increases, everything fades into blue or green. That fading isn't just an artistic choice, it's physics.

That's exactly what Beer-Lambert describes.

In simple terms, it tells you how light gets absorbed as it travels through a medium.

The deeper it goes, the more it loses certain wavelengths. Water absorbs red light much faster than blue, which is why
oceans look blue-green instead of gray.

The equation looks like this:

```math
T(\lambda) = e^{-\sigma(\lambda)\, d}
```

<br />

- $$\lambda$$ &rarr; wavelength, the color of light (in my case `screenColor`); the equation works per wavelength,
  but in code that simply means per RGB channel.
- $$T(\lambda)$$ &rarr; transmittance, how much light makes it through
- $$\sigma(\lambda)$$ &rarr; absorption coefficient, how strongly the medium "eats" each color channel
- $$d$$ &rarr; thickness, how far the light travels through water

In the shader, I keep it channel-wise and a slightly stylized:

```typescript
const sigma = uAbsorptionRGB.mul(uAbsorptionScale); // per-channel, art-directed
const waterThickness = mix(waterDepth, waterDepthRefr, isSafe);
const transmittance = exp(sigma.negate().mul(waterThickness));
const tintColor = uInscatterTint.mul(uInscatterStrength);
const throughWater = mix(tintColor, screenColor, transmittance);
```

<br />

Absorption here is art-directed. I pick my own coefficient per channel, something like `vec3(0.35, 0.10, 0.08)`, so red
fades first, then green, and blue survives the longest. Scaling it with `uAbsorptionScale` lets me slide between a
clear alpine lake and a murky basin without touching the math.

For $$d$$ (thickness), I mix `waterDepth` and `waterDepthRefr` based on the `isSafe` flag. When refraction gives me
a valid distorted UV, I use the refracted thickness; otherwise I fall back to the straight-through one. That keeps the
fade consistent with what's actually visible.

Then I plug these into the Beer-Lambert formula to get transmittance, and blend between a teal-green tint of my choice
(`uInscatterTint`) and my $$\lambda$$ (the screen color) based on that value. The deeper the water, the more light is
absorbed, shifting the mix from background color toward the tint.

<br />

This was the point where everything started to connect: refraction bent the background, and Beer-Lambert gave it
weight and color as it sank into depth.

With that foundation in place, I could finally move on to the bright part: highlights and sun glints, and have
them sit in a scene that finally behaved like water.

<br />

### Sun Glints

With absorption in place, the lake finally had body and depth, but it still missed that last bit of magic: the bright
sparkles that catch your eye.

Those flashes aren't random; they're sunlight reflecting off micro-ripples at just the right angle.
In shader terms, that's just specular reflection, shaped by how tightly aligned the light, surface, and viewer are.

I already had my tangent-space normal, so I used it to craft a new world-space one specifically for highlights, letting
me tweak how tight or spread the sparkles should be.

```typescript
const tsnHighlights = vec3(
  blendedTsn.xy.mul(uHighlightsSpread),
  blendedTsn.z,
).normalize();

const normalHighlights = tsnHighlights.x
  .mul(uTworld)
  .add(tsnHighlights.y.mul(uBworld))
  .add(tsnHighlights.z.mul(uNworld))
  .normalize();
```

Using the same normal for everything makes it physically more accurate but in all honesty it didn't feel right visually.
A smaller scale made the reflections nice and visible and the surface calmer, like a lake should be, but highlights
became dull and condensed more and more into a white circle. A higher scale on the other hand had the opposite effect:
reflections became unrecognizable, but the sun glints looked beautiful.

> Fun fact: this idea to use two separate normal scales for sun glints and the rest came to me during a late night gaming
> session, suddenly, while enjoying the marvellous environment of Ghost of Tsushima. You should check it out, art
> direction is absolutely mind blowing :)

<br />

Then, I reflected the sunlight direction around that adjusted normal, and checked how well it aligned with the camera:

```typescript
const reflectedLight = reflect(uSunDir, normalHighlights);
const align = max(dot(reflectedLight, viewDir), 0);
const spec = pow(align, uShininess);
```

That's the base sparkle, but I didn't want it uniform. The bright spots should lean more toward the horizon, so, once
again, Fresnel enters the chat:

```typescript
const fresnelSpecBoost = mix(1, fresnelSchlick, uHighlightFresnelInfluence);
```

And because deeper areas shouldn't glow the same as shallow ones, I faded it by depth:

```typescript
const highlightsDepthOpacity = smoothstep(
  0,
  uHighlightsDepthOpacityScale,
  waterThickness,
);
```

Finally, the actual sun glint color:

```typescript
const sunGlint = uSunColor
  .mul(spec.mul(uHighlightsGlow).mul(fresnelSpecBoost))
  .mul(highlightsDepthOpacity);
```

<br />

The result? The surface finally came alive: small, sharp bursts of light dancing with every ripple, fading naturally
with depth and angle.

It's one of those touches that doesn't need to be scientifically perfect; it just needs to feel right.

And now, with reflection, refraction, absorption, and glints all in place, the water finally looked believable,
not perfect, but grounded, like it belonged in the world instead of floating on top of it.

<br />

### Opacity by distance

Even with absorption in place, close-up water can look too present—like a tinted sheet. I wanted two things: 1. see clearly when I'm right at the shoreline (or when I fall in), and 2. let the surface “close up” with distance so the far lake reads as a coherent body.

I solved it with a simple screen-space fade driven by horizontal distance (XZ) from the camera:

```typescript
// Distance in the horizontal plane (avoid sqrt by using squared distance)
const distanceXZSquared = dot(
  positionWorld.xz.sub(cameraPosition.xz),
  positionWorld.xz.sub(cameraPosition.xz),
);

// Pre-square the thresholds so smoothstep compares squared quantities
const min2 = uniforms.uMinDist.mul(uniforms.uMinDist);
const max2 = uniforms.uMaxDist.mul(uniforms.uMaxDist);

// Soft ramp from near to far, plus a floor so water never fully disappears
const opacity = smoothstep(min2, max2, distanceXZSquared)
  .add(uniforms.uMinOpacity)
  .clamp();

// Bring it all together
const shadedWater = mix(throughWater, reflectedColor, fresnelWeight);
const color = mix(screenColor, shadedWater, opacity);
this.colorNode = color.add(sunGlint);
```

Why XZ only?

I only fade with planar distance so the effect is stable as camera height changes (standing on a bank vs. flying a bit higher). It reads like “the farther the lake surface, the less you see through it,” which matches how we perceive real water at a glance.

What each knob does

- `uMinDist / uMaxDist` &rarr; where the fade starts and fully kicks in (in world units). These are squared internally
  for the comparison, which avoids a costly `sqrt`.
- `uMinOpacity` &rarr; a floor so nearby water doesn't disappear completely. Handy when you don't yet have
  buoyancy/underwater rendering; you still get a hint of surface even right next to the camera.

How it layers with the rest

- The base is `screenColor` (undistorted scene.)
- `throughWater` (Beer-Lambert + inscatter) tints and attenuates what's behind the surface.
- `reflectedColor` (cubemap) comes in via Fresnel, stronger at glancing angles.
- This `opacity` then decides how much we lean away from the raw scene into the shaded water look as distance grows.

It's a cheap trick, but a useful one. By fading opacity with distance, the water feels clearer up close while still
reading as a solid body farther away. And when the player ends up underwater, they don't get lost in murky Beer-Lambert
tinting, you can actually see them moving beneath the surface.

### Outcome

Piece by piece, the shader started to take shape.

- The normals gave the surface ripples.
- Depth gave it body.
- Refraction bent the world behind it.
- Reflections and Fresnel added realism at the viewing angle.
- Highlights and glints brought sparkle to the surface.
- Beer-Lambert absorption gave the water its characteristic tint.
- And finally, opacity by distance balanced clarity near the player with solidity farther away.

Each step on its own is simple enough, but together they stack into something that feels alive.

At this stage, the shader is far from "finished", there's always another layer to add, another refinement to try.
But for me, this is the first time it really started to look like the water I had imagined from the beginning.
