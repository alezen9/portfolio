## Chapter III: Beyond Ripples

Months passed before I came back to the lake again.

The spark this time wasn't my own, it came from a [post on X](https://x.com/psergiomr/status/1899840041288917427) which
completely stunned me. Its water shimmered and breathed in a way I hadn't even imagined for Revo. I bookmarked it,
walked away, and told myself: _one day_.

When I did return, I'd just spent some time tinkering in Godot, playing with stylized water shaders and soaking up
whatever knowledge I could find. But Revo was still my main playground, so I carried what I'd learned back into
Three.js and kept building there.

I wanted to capture the mood of that post, but in my own way.

What followed wasn't one breakthrough, but a stack of small pieces, each filling in part of the picture: normals to
shape the ripples, depth to give the water body, refraction to bend the world beneath, reflections and Fresnel to tie
the surface to the sky, highlights and glints to make it sparkle, Beer-Lambert absorption for color and weight, and a
simple opacity trick to balance clarity up close with solidity at a distance.

Each one could be its own story. Together they layered into something that finally felt like the lake I'd pictured
from the start.

<br />

### Surface Normals & Waves

The surface is always the starting point. No matter what tricks come later: depth falloff, absorption, reflections, if
the ripples don't feel alive, the illusion collapses.

This version reused the normal maps from Chapter II, but with a twist. I sampled the same water normal twice, at
different scales and scroll directions, then blended them using RNM, which preserves directionality and detail
instead of giving the mushy look of naive interpolation.

<br />

```typescript
class WaterMaterial extends MeshBasicNodeMaterial {
  // ...
  private sampleNormal = Fn(([uv = vec2(0)]) => {
    const tex = texture(waterNormalTexture, uv);
    return tex.mul(2).sub(1).rgb.normalize();
  });

  private createMaterial() {
    // ...
    const nUV1 = uv().add(frequency).mul(uUvScale.mul(1.37)).fract();
    const tsn1 = this.sampleNormal(nUV1);
    const nUV2 = uv().sub(frequency).mul(uUvScale.mul(0.73)).fract();
    const tsn2 = this.sampleNormal(nUV2);
    const blendedTsn = utils.blendRNM(tsn1, tsn2);
    const tsn = vec3(blendedTsn.xy.mul(uNormalScale), blendedTsn.z).normalize();
    const normal = tsn.x
      .mul(uTworld)
      .add(tsn.y.mul(uBworld))
      .add(tsn.z.mul(uNworld))
      .normalize();
    // ...
  }
}
```

The odd multipliers (`1.37` and `0.73`) aren't random. If I'd picked clean ratios like `0.5` and `1.25`, the scrolls
would eventually sync up and reveal a repeating pattern. Using awkward, almost-irrational numbers keeps the two layers
drifting forever without tiling in lockstep, a dirt-cheap way to break repetition.

Another small but important detail: I only scale the X and Y components of the tangent-space normal (`tsn`).
Those represent sideways tilt, the part that makes ripples catch the light. Scaling Z (the "up" axis) would just squash
the surface flat and kill the contrast. It's a subtle distinction, but it makes a big difference in how natural the
water feels.

And finally, blending itself. In my earlier attempt I just averaged two normals with `mix(n1, n2, 0.5)`. It looked fine
at a glance, but it washed out the detail and left the surface feeling flat. RNM (Reoriented Normal Mapping) avoids
that by reorienting one normal into the other's space before combining, so the fine detail survives. If you're curious,
Self Shadow's [article](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/blending-in-detail/) is the gold standard breakdown of
RNM, UDN, and other variants.

<br />

### Depth

Normals gave the surface life, but the lake still felt hollow, like ripples painted on glass.

What I needed was depth. Not in the everyday sense of how far something sinks under water, but in 3D graphics terms:
**how far each fragment is from the camera**.

In Three.js TSL, the entry point is `viewportDepthTexture(uv)`. That gives you the value from the depth buffer, but it
isn't in world units, it's in clip space. After projection, X, Y, and Z all get squeezed into a normalized cube (NDC,
or Normalized Device Coordinates).

Here's where it gets tricky: different APIs disagree on the Z range. WebGL/OpenGL use `[-1..1]`, while WebGPU (along
with DirectX and Metal) use `[0..1]`. A tiny detail, but if you ignore it, the math falls apart.

I learned that the hard way: everything looked perfect in Chrome with WebGPU... until I opened Firefox (WebGL2) and
my lake collapsed into nonsense.

<br />

**From clip space to linear depth**

The goal here is "linear" depth: the actual camera-space distance.

The usual way to recover linear depth is to take the fragment's position in clip space and multiply it by the
inverse projection matrix. That brings it back into view space, and from there you can grab the Z value as the true
distance.

It works, but it felt like overkill, a full 4&times;4 matrix multiplication per fragment, just to get one number.

While digging around, I came across [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NCXr8zrT5zs) excellent video that showed a
cleaner path. Since I only needed the Z, the math collapses: we can cherry-pick just two elements of the projection
matrix (I called them p3z and p2z) and compute it with a single division:

```math
z_{linear} = \frac{p_{3z}}{z_{ndc} + p_{2z}}
```

That shortcut spared me from pushing an entire inverse matrix into the shader and suddenly made depth cheap enough
to use everywhere.

Here's what it looks like in my shader:

```typescript
//...
const zNdc = viewportDepthTexture(screenUV).r;
const isWebGL = float(1).sub(uniforms.uIsWebGPU);
const webglDepth = zNdc.mul(2).sub(1).mul(isWebGL);
const webgpuDepth = zNdc.mul(uniforms.uIsWebGPU);
const sceneDepth = webglDepth.add(webgpuDepth);
const p3z = cameraProjectionMatrix.element(3).element(2);
const p2z = cameraProjectionMatrix.element(2).element(2);
const sceneLinear = p3z.div(sceneDepth.add(p2z));
const fragLinear = positionView.z.negate();
const isUnderWater = step(fragLinear, sceneLinear);
const fragmentDepth = sceneLinear.sub(fragLinear);
const waterDepth = fragmentDepth.div(uniforms.uDepthDistance).clamp();
//...
```

Three.js doesn't expose a reliable flag for WebGPU vs WebGL, even though it adapts internally, so I hooked up my own.
By checking `navigator.gpu?.requestAdapter()`, I set a uniform `uIsWebGPU`, and used that to drive whether I treat
depth as `[0..1]` or `[-1..1]`.

Branching (`if/else`) would also work, but GPUs are happiest when every thread follows the same path; even a tiny
conditional can slow things down. Driving it with a uniform avoids that cost.

Another important detail: Three.js matrices are column-major (like GLSL). If you're cross-checking OpenGL docs, which
often show row-major, the indices appear shifted. For example, what looks like row 3, col 2 in the docs is actually
`.element(2).element(3)` in Three.js. That mismatch tripped me until I realized it was just layout, not math.

If you want to dive deeper into how projection matrices shape this math, two resources helped me a lot:

- [WebGPU fundamentals — perspective projection](https://webgpufundamentals.org/webgpu/lessons/webgpu-perspective-projection.html)
- [Song Ho's OpenGL projection matrix breakdown](https://www.songho.ca/opengl/gl_projectionmatrix.html)

<br />

Depth was one of those invisible victories. At this point, I wasn't even using it for anything fancy yet, but I knew
it was going to be a cornerstone for multiple effects. It drives how strong refraction bends the background, how quickly
light is absorbed with distance, even how the surface should fade near the shoreline. Without depth, all of
those are blind guesses.

<br />

### Refraction

So far, we've prepared two important ingredients: surface normals (ripples) and depth values. But at this point,
they're just sitting there. Normals aren't affecting shading, and depth is just a number in the buffer. Nothing
changes visually.

The first place where they really come alive is refraction.

When you look at real water, what's underneath doesn't appear perfectly aligned. The world bends and warps with
the ripples. That's refraction: light changing direction as it passes through water. Shallow spots look almost
undistorted, but the deeper the water, the stronger the bend.

That's where depth comes in, it drives how much distortion to apply. Shallow water keeps the image sharp, while
deeper water exaggerates the wobble.

To pull this off, we need another piece of the puzzle: the ==screen texture== (in TSL called viewportTexture). Think
of it like a screenshot of the scene, stored as a texture you can sample with UVs. Instead of fetching from an image
on disk, you're sampling the already-rendered scene.

Shift those screen UVs by the ripple normals, and suddenly the surface is no longer cosmetic. What's behind the water
bends and dances with the waves, just like in the real world.

```typescript
const distortionStrength = mix(
  uniforms.uRefractionStrength,
  uniforms.uRefractionStrength.mul(1.5),
  waterDepth,
);

const distortion = tsn.xy.mul(distortionStrength); // tangent space: xy is sideways tilt
const refractedScreenUv = screenUV.add(distortion.mul(isUnderWater));
```

A couple of details worth unpacking:

**Why tangent space XY?**

The normal map gives me ripples in tangent space. The X and Y components are sideways "tilts" that tell me how much
the surface is sloping horizontally. That's exactly the direction I want for UV distortion. Using Z wouldn't make
sense, that's the "up" axis.

<br />

**Depth-based scaling**

Notice how I don't use a constant distortion. The deeper the fragment, the stronger the bending. That's why I
interpolate between `uRefractionStrength` and `1.5 x uRefractionStrength`. Without that, shallow areas would wobble
just as much as deep areas, which feels wrong. The min and max refraction strength here are arbitrary.

<br />

**Why not just stop there?**
If I only distorted the screen color, refraction would already look okay. But there's a catch: what happens if the
distortion pushes my UV into an area that's supposed to be behind geometry? Suddenly, I'm sampling the wrong pixel.

That's why I re-sample depth at the distorted UV:

```typescript
const depthRefr = viewportDepthTexture(refractedScreenUv).r;
const sceneLinearRefr = p3z.div(depthRefr.add(p2z));
const isSafe = step(fragLinear, sceneLinearRefr);
const safeScreenUv = mix(screenUV, refractedScreenUv, isSafe);
const screenColor = viewportTexture(safeScreenUv).rgb;
```

That `isSafe` mask prevents me from pulling colors from places that don't actually exist in front of the camera. Without
it, you get ugly artifacts, stretched pixels or seeing "through" geometry you shouldn't.

<br />

**Performance note**
Refraction is deceptively expensive: each distorted UV means another screen and depth sample. For a big body of water
that covers a lot of pixels, this adds up fast. That's why I keep the distortion subtle and only sample once.

<br />

This sets up Beer-Lambert absorption nicely, because now you've established both the base distortion and the need to
measure depth again at refracted UVs.

<br />

### Reflections, Fresnel & Viewing Angle

With distortion in place, the water finally felt alive, but it was still missing one of the most recognizable cues:
reflections.

When you look at a lake, sometimes it's perfectly clear, and sometimes all you see is the sky or the world mirrored
back at you. That shift isn't random, it depends on the viewing angle. Look straight down and you see through;
look toward the horizon and the surface starts to act like a mirror.

To capture this, I made use of two things: a cubemap for reflections, and Fresnel for weighting them.

<br />

**Reflections from the environment**

The cubemap is a simple trick: sample the environment texture with a reflection vector. The reflection vector is
just the view direction reflected across the surface normal.

```typescript
const viewDir = normalize(cameraPosition.sub(positionWorld));
const reflectVector = reflect(viewDir.negate(), normal);
const reflectedColor = cubeTexture(assetManager.envMapTexture, reflectVector);
```

This gives us the basic reflection color, but if applied everywhere equally, it looks wrong. Water would always
behave like a mirror, even when you're looking straight down.

<br />

**Fresnel to the rescue**

That's where Fresnel comes in. The Fresnel effect describes how reflectivity increases as the viewing angle becomes
more glancing (close to parallel to the surface).

The classic approximation is Schlick's formula:

```math
F(\theta) = F_0 + (1 - F_0) (1 - \cos\theta)^5
```

- `F0` is the reflectivity at head-on (usually very low for water, ~2%)
- `cosθ` is the dot between the surface normal and the view direction
- the exponent `5` isn't arbitrary, it comes from a common approximation (Schlick's), which uses `^5` as a balance
  between accuracy and efficiency. In reality, Fresnel is derived from more complex equations involving polarization
  and material IOR, but Schlick's `^5` form gets close enough while being dirt cheap for real-time graphics.

In code:

```typescript
const cosTheta = dot(normal, viewDir).clamp();
const F0 = float(0.02);
const grazingAngle = float(1.0).sub(cosTheta);
const grazingAnglePow5 = grazingAngle
  .mul(grazingAngle)
  .mul(grazingAngle)
  .mul(grazingAngle)
  .mul(grazingAngle);

const fresnelSchlick = F0.add(float(1.0).sub(F0).mul(grazingAnglePow5));
const fresnelWeight = fresnelSchlick.mul(uniforms.uFresnelScale).clamp();
```

The result is that reflections fade in naturally at grazing angles while letting through-water effects dominate when
you're looking straight down.

<br />

**Why this matters**
Fresnel is one of those subtle effects that the brain notices immediately when it's missing. Without it, water either
looks like a sheet of glass or a perfect mirror. With it, the balance shifts naturally depending on your angle, and
suddenly the reflections feel right.

It is not just for blending reflections, though. Later, I also use it to boost highlights and glints on the surface,
giving them that extra sparkle when the sun hits at just the right angle.

<br />

### Highlights & Glints

If reflections give water its mirror-like character, highlights are the little sparks that make it feel alive.
Think about a lake under the sun: you don't just see a vague bright patch, you see thousands of tiny glints dancing across the surface.

In shaders, this comes from specular highlights. The idea is simple: when the direction of incoming light (the sun) reflects off the surface and happens to align with your view direction, you get a sharp spike of brightness.

In code, it looks like this:

```typescript
const reflectedLight = reflect(uniforms.uSunDir, normal);
const align = max(dot(reflectedLight, viewDir), 0.0);
const spec = pow(align, uniforms.uShininess);

const fresnelSpecBoost = mix(
  float(1),
  fresnelSchlick,
  uniforms.uHighlightFresnelInfluence,
);

const sunGlint = uniforms.uSunColor.mul(
  spec.mul(uniforms.uHighlightsGlow).mul(fresnelSpecBoost),
);
```

A few key pieces are at play here:

<br />

**Shininess**

The `pow()` call controls how "tight" the highlight is. A low shininess spreads the highlight into a wide, soft glow.
A high shininess value makes it razor-sharp, like a sparkle on a calm surface. For my water I keep it high (hundreds),
because I wanted those crisp glints rather than a smeared gloss.

<br />

**Glow factor**

Water rarely looks right if the highlight is just a tiny pixel spike. I multiply it by a `uHighlightsGlow` uniform to
give the glint some visual weight. This makes the bright spots more visible without flooding the surface.

<br />

**Fresnel boost**

Highlights also depend on the viewing angle. By blending in some of the Fresnel weight, I make the glints more
pronounced at grazing angles, where reflections dominate. The difference is subtle, but it prevents highlights from
feeling flat or pasted on.

<br />

**Why bother if reflections already exist?**

Because reflections alone tend to look static and dull. Specular glints are what trick the eye into believing the
surface is alive and interacting with light. Without them, the water feels painted on. With them, it suddenly shimmers.

<br />

### Beer-Lambert: Depth-Based Absorption

So far, the water surface bends the world with ripples, shifts what you see with refraction, and sprinkles light with
highlights. But something is still missing: color.

If you've ever looked into a lake, you know the water isn't truly transparent. Shallow parts look almost clear, but as
depth increases, the world below fades into blue or green. That fading isn't just an artistic choice, it's physics.

This is where the Beer-Lambert law comes in.

In simple terms, Beer-Lambert describes how light is absorbed as it travels through a medium. The deeper it goes,
the more it loses certain wavelengths. Water absorbs red light more aggressively than blue, which is why oceans look
blue-green instead of gray.

The formula looks like this:

```math
T(\lambda) = e^{-\sigma(\lambda) \cdot d}
```

Where:

- `T(λ)` is the transmittance &rarr; how much light survives) at wavelength `λ`
- `σ(λ)` is the absorption coefficient &rarr; how much the medium eats each color channel
- `d` is the thickness of the medium &rarr; the distance light has to travel through water

In code:

```typescript
const sigma = uniforms.uAbsorptionRGB.mul(uniforms.uAbsorptionScale);
const transmittance = exp(sigma.negate().mul(waterThickness));

const absorbedColor = screenColor.mul(transmittance);
const fillColor = uniforms.uInscatterTint
  .mul(float(1.0).sub(transmittance))
  .mul(uniforms.uInscatterStrength);

const throughWater = absorbedColor.add(fillColor);
```

A breakdown:

- Absorbed color: I take the screen color (what's behind the water) and darken it according to transmittance. This mimics how reds fade
  faster, greens slower, and blues slowest.
- Fill color: if you only darken, everything just looks murky. Real water glows a little because of scattered light.
  That's where the tint (`uInscatterTint`) comes in: it adds back some color as a compensation. Think of it as water's
  haze.
- Through water: combine both, and you get a balance: background objects darken with depth, while the water itself
  contributes its own subtle tint.

  <br />

**Why absorption matters**

Without this step, deep water would look like perfectly clear glass. Refraction alone bends the image, but the colors
beneath stay too clean. Beer-Lambert gives water its weight, the sense that light is struggling the further it travels.

<br />

**Art direction knobs**
The absorption coefficients are purely artistic in my shader. For example, I used something like:

```typescript
uAbsorptionRGB = vec3(0.35, 0.1, 0.08);
```

That means red gets eaten fastest, while blue lingers the most — exactly the bias you'd expect from natural water.
Scaling those numbers up or down lets me choose between crystal-clear lakes or murky green swamps.

<br />

**Why it matters**

Without absorption, water never feels deep. Refraction bends images, but Beer-Lambert gives water its weight. It
explains why a shallow puddle shows every pebble beneath, while a lake swallows them whole.

<br />

### Opacity by distance

Even with absorption in place, close-up water can look too present—like a tinted sheet. I wanted two things: 1. see clearly when I'm right at the shoreline (or when I fall in), and 2. let the surface “close up” with distance so the far lake reads as a coherent body.

I solved it with a simple screen-space fade driven by horizontal distance (XZ) from the camera:

```typescript
// Distance in the horizontal plane (avoid sqrt by using squared distance)
const distanceXZSquared = dot(
  positionWorld.xz.sub(cameraPosition.xz),
  positionWorld.xz.sub(cameraPosition.xz),
);

// Pre-square the thresholds so smoothstep compares squared quantities
const min2 = uniforms.uMinDist.mul(uniforms.uMinDist);
const max2 = uniforms.uMaxDist.mul(uniforms.uMaxDist);

// Soft ramp from near to far, plus a floor so water never fully disappears
const opacity = smoothstep(min2, max2, distanceXZSquared)
  .add(uniforms.uMinOpacity)
  .clamp();

// Bring it all together
const shadedWater = mix(throughWater, reflectedColor, fresnelWeight);
const color = mix(screenColor, shadedWater, opacity);
this.colorNode = color.add(sunGlint);
```

Why XZ only?

I only fade with planar distance so the effect is stable as camera height changes (standing on a bank vs. flying a bit higher). It reads like “the farther the lake surface, the less you see through it,” which matches how we perceive real water at a glance.

What each knob does

- `uMinDist / uMaxDist` &rarr; where the fade starts and fully kicks in (in world units). These are squared internally
  for the comparison, which avoids a costly `sqrt`.
- `uMinOpacity` &rarr; a floor so nearby water doesn't disappear completely. Handy when you don't yet have
  buoyancy/underwater rendering; you still get a hint of surface even right next to the camera.

How it layers with the rest

- The base is `screenColor` (undistorted scene.)
- `throughWater` (Beer-Lambert + inscatter) tints and attenuates what's behind the surface.
- `reflectedColor` (cubemap) comes in via Fresnel, stronger at glancing angles.
- This `opacity` then decides how much we lean away from the raw scene into the shaded water look as distance grows.

It's a cheap trick, but a useful one. By fading opacity with distance, the water feels clearer up close while still
reading as a solid body farther away. And when the player ends up underwater, they don't get lost in murky Beer-Lambert
tinting, you can actually see them moving beneath the surface.

### Outcome

Piece by piece, the shader started to take shape.

- The normals gave the surface ripples.
- Depth gave it body.
- Refraction bent the world behind it.
- Reflections and Fresnel added realism at the viewing angle.
- Highlights and glints brought sparkle to the surface.
- Beer-Lambert absorption gave the water its characteristic tint.
- And finally, opacity by distance balanced clarity near the player with solidity farther away.

Each step on its own is simple enough, but together they stack into something that feels alive.

At this stage, the shader is far from "finished", there's always another layer to add, another refinement to try.
But for me, this is the first time it really started to look like the water I had imagined from the beginning.
