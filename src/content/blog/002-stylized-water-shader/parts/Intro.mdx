Faking things is at the heart of game development.

The goal isn't to perfectly simulate reality, but to make something that looks right, feels right, and fits
the world it belongs to. Water is no exception.

There are countless great guides on water—stylized ponds, film-ready oceans—each with its own clever tricks.
This isn't meant to compete with them. It's simply my path: piecing together what I knew, adapting ideas to
my world, and seeing what felt true on screen.

In this article I walk through how my shader evolved. It began with simple vertex displacement, picked up detail
with normal maps, and eventually learned refraction, reflections, and a few small cheats. Each version taught
me something new and moved me closer to water that felt alive inside
[Revo Realms](https://revo-realms.aleksandargjoreski.dev), my Three.js playground where I test ideas,
learn graphics programming, and slowly stitch together an environment that feels alive.

As you can see, the difference is pretty striking, but it didn't happen overnight.

Each version was a small step forward, built on experiments, mistakes, and whatever bits of
knowledge I managed to piece together.

I've always been fascinated by water, in life and in games. It's an extraordinary substance: essential for survival,
an unstoppable force of nature, yet also deeply calming, whether it falls from the sky in drops or rests quietly
in the hands of the earth.

Re-creating that feeling in 3D is a challenge. With limited resources, water can easily look flat or artificial,
but when it works, it often becomes one of the most striking parts of a scene.

Rivers, streams, ponds, oceans, lakes... I had many options when deciding what kind of water Revo should have.
At first I wavered between a flowing stream and a lake, but in the end, the lake felt right.

Almost unconsciously, I shaped it to resemble the small one in Masere Park ([see on Google Maps](https://maps.app.goo.gl/yQj11xUyxqRMamBt6)),
in Pelugo, the village in Northern Italy where I grew up.

Like any story, this one too begins with a simple step.
