## One step beyond: cascaded shadow maps

The next stop was the one I had already tried and failed with: CSM, **cascaded shadow maps**. This time I wanted to understand it
before judging it.

The idea is to split the camera's view by distance. The ground close to the camera belongs to a near slice, the middle distance to
a second one, the far hills to a third. Each slice gets its own shadow map, fitted around the part of the view it covers.

```text
distance from camera:   | near      | middle    | far       |
shadow map:             | map A     | map B     | map C     |
ground it covers:       | small     | bigger    | biggest   |
detail per metre:       | finest    | less      | least     |
```

{/* illustration S2: top down. The camera frustum as a triangle cut into three slices, near, middle and far. Around each slice a square shadow map (A, B, C), each one bigger than the previous, drawn with the same texel grid count so the texels grow with the square. */}

The near map covers a small area, so it gets a lot of texels per metre exactly where you look the most. The far map covers much
more ground with bigger texels, which is fine because you can't see fine detail that far anyway. When a point needs its shadow, its
distance from the camera picks the map.

It's a very sensible answer to the resolution problem, and it's what a lot of games shipped with for years.

<br />

It has its own quirks though. Where two slices meet, the two maps have different texel sizes, so a shadow crossing the boundary can
visibly change quality or show a seam. You can blend between them, but that means sampling both near the boundary. A map fitted too
tightly to the view can also crawl and shimmer as the camera moves, and stabilizing it usually means giving up some of that tight
fit.

And the car from the previous chapter is still there. A cascade fitted to what the camera sees still needs every caster that can
throw a shadow into it, visible or not.

The part that bothered me the most is simpler: every cascade is a complete shadow map, re-rendered as the camera moves. The far one
spends a lot of texels on parts of its view nobody is looking at.

Three.js has both a [CSM addon for WebGL](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/CSM.html) and a
[CSMShadowNode for WebGPU](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/CSMShadowNode.html). Looking back at my first attempt, they give you the
machinery, but choosing the splits, the bias and the caster setup is still up to you. I most likely got at least one of those wrong
back then, which makes that old experiment a pretty bad argument against cascades.

By the time I got here my question had changed anyway. It wasn't "how many maps can I afford" anymore, it was "how much of each map
do I actually need?"
