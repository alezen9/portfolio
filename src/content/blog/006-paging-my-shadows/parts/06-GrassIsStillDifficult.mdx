### Grass is still difficult

Grass was one of the main reasons I wanted real shadows in the first place. I wanted a hill or a tree to shade the grass growing on
it, not just the terrain underneath. That part works now, and it looks lovely.

I also wanted every blade to cast its own little shadow. That part is still open.

Before the paged system I tried a separate 2048 map just for the grass. Over the area it covered, each texel was roughly 8 to 11
centimetres, so a thin blade either missed the map entirely or became a dark dot, and the wind made the dots crawl around.

With virtual pages I had millimetres of detail up close, so I tried putting the blades into the moving pages. And they did cast! The
result was a field of sharp, noisy speckles that got worse with every gust. Higher resolution doesn't automatically mean a nicer
picture.

It wasn't cheap either. In one test on my M2 Pro with the camera standing still, the whole frame took 5.04 ms on the GPU without grass casting
and 7.16 ms with it. A performance pass brought that down to 6.04 ms, still a lot for something that looked worse. One blob shadow
per clump instead of per blade cost 5.69 ms and looked exactly like what it was, a field of blobs.

<br />

### What the big studios do

I got curious about how games with a lot of grass deal with this, and the answer seems to be: mostly they don't draw every blade into
the shadow map either.

Bend Studio shipped _Days Gone_ with **screen space shadows** for the sun, marching through the depth buffer toward the light to
catch small details the shadow map misses, and they later
[published the code](https://www.bendstudio.com/blog/inside-bend-screen-space-shadows/) with a talk. The PC version of _Ghost of
Tsushima_ also has a separate screen space shadows setting described as adding foliage shadows that the shadow maps miss.

So I tried exactly that. It cost about 0.8 ms, and the result was mush: soft dark smudges that didn't read as blade shadows at all.
That one got removed too.

<br />

### Where it landed

For now grass and flowers **receive** shadows but don't cast their own.

They are also **soft receivers**. Instead of the page level their distance would normally pick, they read coarser levels, dithered
between two neighbours, so the shadow of a hill or a tree falls across the blades as a smooth gradient instead of breaking into
sharp fragments. The ground underneath gets a softened treatment too.

The pine canopy ended up with the same treatment much later. Its needles are thin, noisy and alpha tested, and up close they were
asking for pages all over the pool. Reading coarser levels fixed both the noise and the page count, and honestly it looks even
better than before.

It leaves some contact detail on the table, but it keeps the part of the screen I stare at the most calm and clean. I still want
proper blade shadows someday. I just haven't found a version that looks calm in motion and is worth its cost.

It reminded me of the [grass culling](/blog/trimming-my-grass-shader/) story. Understanding a technique and liking the image it
produces are two very different things.
