### From a bake to a cache

I didn't go from Blender bakes straight to the system I have now. It took a few detours.

The first step was much more modest: a cache of ordinary directional shadow textures that scrolled with the player, which the
terrain and the grass sampled, plus two small cascades for moving things. Moving an object or the sun just rebuilt the cache. After
a year of exporting and packing textures by hand, that alone already felt like magic.

It also left me with different shadows for different receivers, and anything that needed both, like the top of the statue's
pedestal, kept finding a connection I had forgotten. I had traded the bake ritual for a collection of special cases.

<br />

### The first version

So I started on pages. The first version had one goal: make it work.

And it did, eventually. September 20th was the first time a paged shadow showed up on screen, and I was ridiculously happy about
it. Then I kept adding things: a static page cache, the pine canopy, the grass, the flowers. Each one fixed something and each one
added a little patch on top of the previous ones.

That's also when things started going wrong. Pages flickered like crazy. Getting the quality right without the cost going wild felt
like a balancing act I was losing. Shadows showed up where they shouldn't, didn't show up where they should, and cache invalidation
kept not doing what it was supposed to do. There's a reason it's on that famous list of the hardest things in programming, together
with naming things and off-by-one errors :)

At some point I seriously considered going back to baking.

What kept me going was a bit more research, which led me to clipmaps. I wasn't using them yet, and once I understood how nested
levels around the camera would take care of a lot of what I was fighting, going back felt like quitting one step before it got
good.

<br />

### Starting over

The second version started from scratch.

This time I knew how the pieces were supposed to connect, so I wrote the plan first and thought about the architecture instead of
growing it one patch at a time. Clipmap levels around the camera, casters split into small clusters of triangles, and the page
depths filled by a compute shader instead of the regular raster pipeline (more on that in a moment).

From being fed up to having virtual shadow maps working properly took about two and a half weeks of implementation, on top of the
reading I did before.

Not everything was smooth in the second round either. My favourite bug from this one: some shadows appeared out of nowhere near
moving objects, as if there were invisible casters walking around. The timings looked fine and the code looked fine. I only found it
by reading the WGSL that TSL generated from my code, where a value shared between two branches had been cached into a variable that
only one branch ever assigned. Everywhere else it read zero, which meant zero bias and black patches.

Lesson learned: after restructuring shader code, look at the image, not just at the numbers.

The rest of the article is how that second version works today.
