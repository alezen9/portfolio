## Classic shadow maps

I had used shadow maps for more than a year without ever really looking inside one, so this is where I started.

The idea is surprisingly simple. Render the scene once from the light's point of view, but instead of colors you store distances:
for every pixel of that render, how far the light travelled before hitting something. Then render the scene normally from the
camera, and for every visible point ask one question: was something closer to the light along the same ray?

If yes, that point is in shadow. That's it, that's the whole trick.

<br />

The example that helped me the most: a car parked on a hill above a valley. The camera looks at the valley floor and the car is
just outside the picture. Its shadow still falls into the valley.

The camera render alone can't know that, because the car isn't in it. The render from the sun can.

```text
one ray of sunlight, seen from the sun:

sun  ->  car on the hill  ->  valley floor
         stored distance      distance of the point I am shading
         (closer)             (farther)

the camera sees the valley, not the car
the comparison says something blocked the sun
```

{/* illustration S1: side view. Sun top left with parallel rays going down to the right. A hill on the left with a car on it, a valley floor on the right. The camera frustum looks only at the valley, the car is outside it. One ray highlighted: it hits the car first (label "stored depth"), continues and reaches point P on the valley (label "P's depth"). P marked as shadowed. */}

For a sun, all the rays are parallel, so the light's view is orthographic. Nothing shrinks with distance like it does through the
camera. Every texel of the map remembers the first surface its ray met. In one place that is the car, next to it the hillside,
somewhere else the valley floor.

When the camera later draws the valley, each visible point gets projected into that map and compared with what's stored there. If
the stored surface is closer to the sun, the point is in shadow.

<br />

There is one detail in there that ended up mattering a lot later: the camera decides which surfaces I want to shade, not which
objects can cast onto them. If I only drew the objects the camera can see into the shadow map, the car would vanish from the light's
view and its shadow would vanish from the valley.

So the shadow pass needs its own idea of what matters. Keep that car in mind, it comes back :)

<br />

### Where it starts hurting

A texture full of distances has a few well known problems.

The first one is **shadow acne**. A surface can end up comparing against a slightly different version of its own depth and decide
it is shadowing itself, which shows up as ugly dark stripes where everything should be lit. The fix is a small **bias** that pushes
the comparison a bit, but push too much and the shadow detaches from the object that casts it. That is the "Peter Panning" look,
where things seem to float a little above the ground.

The second one is jagged edges. A single comparison gives you a hard yes or no, so the edge of a shadow is as blocky as the texels
of the map. The usual fix is to do several comparisons around the point and average the results. The important part is averaging
the comparisons, not the depths, because averaging a car's depth with the valley's depth invents a surface that doesn't exist.

The third one, and the one that really matters in a world like Revo, is resolution.

A 4096 x 4096 map covering about 512 metres gives each texel roughly 12.5 centimetres of ground. That's fine for a distant hillside
and hopeless for the contact shadow under the player. Doubling the resolution quadruples the memory and still only halves the texel
size. Keep going until grass blades and small props look right and you are paying for every empty or distant corner of the map too.

You can fit the map tightly around the player and get great nearby shadows, but then the valley further away falls outside of it.
Or you cover the whole world and everything nearby gets blurry and blocky.

That was pretty much the exact trade I had been fighting with Three.js's built-in shadows. What I needed was a way to spend
resolution where it would actually be seen.
