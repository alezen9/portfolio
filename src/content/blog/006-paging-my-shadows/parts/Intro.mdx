If you scroll through [Revo](https://revo-realms.aleksandargjoreski.dev/)'s git history you will find one commit message that keeps
coming back for about a year: `update shadowmap`.

Every single one of them is the same little ritual. Change something in Blender, bake the shadows onto the terrain, export a PNG,
pack it together with the other textures, compress it to KTX2, load it in the project and make sure whatever needed that shadow was
reading the right texture.

If the object had to shadow itself, like the Goku statue, that was another pair of shoes. It needed its own bake on its own
texture, at a resolution high enough not to look blocky, and then the whole export, pack and convert dance started again.

I had a few scripts to automate the packing and converting, so none of it was hard. It was just long, and it was waiting for me
every single time I touched the scene.

It never strictly stopped me from adding things or moving them around. It did make me hesitate though. I would look at a rock,
think it would look better a couple of metres to the left, remember what moving it meant, and leave it exactly where it was.

Changing the light direction or its color was even worse, because that meant rebaking everything. And baked shadows are static by
definition, so anything that moved needed its own solution anyway.

For a project I use as a sandbox to experiment freely, that is a funny constraint to accept :)

<br />

It didn't start with baking. Like a lot of people who learned Three.js through [Three.js Journey](https://threejs-journey.com/), I
began with the built-in shadow map: a directional light, `castShadow`, `receiveShadow` and a shadow camera following the player
around.

It is a great way to start. At some point though it starts feeling limiting, especially when performance is non-negotiable and the
world keeps getting bigger. With a larger area and a camera the player can rotate freely, I was either losing quality or paying too
much for it.

I also tried cascaded shadow maps early on. I honestly can't remember the details, only that performance was not great and that I
most likely misconfigured it, because I was using it without really understanding what it was doing.

So I went back to baking, with a tiny shadow map just for the player. Later I got rid of even that and gave the player an analytical
shadow instead. It's a sphere, after all, and the math for a sphere is friendly.

That setup survived for a long time, until a few weeks ago I got properly fed up with it.

Since the start I've been going for a cozy, golden hour kind of mood, and Revo is slowly growing into a world with a lot more
vegetation and life in it. I use it to see how far I can push both how it looks and how fast it runs. Baked shadows were cheap, but
every new thing meant another bake. I wanted to be able to move the sun or add a caster without planning a texture workflow around
it first.

<br />

This time I wanted to actually understand shadows. How they work in the most basic, classic setup, how the industry kept optimizing
the problem over the years and what the big engines do today. The answer to that last question was something called virtual
shadow maps, a term I had never heard before.

Most of the learning happened in extra long conversations with AI, going back and forth until a concept finally made sense, plus
YouTube videos whenever I needed someone to draw the thing for me.

Understanding it conceptually was not trivial, but I eventually got there. Implementing it was a whole different story, and I ended
up building it twice.
