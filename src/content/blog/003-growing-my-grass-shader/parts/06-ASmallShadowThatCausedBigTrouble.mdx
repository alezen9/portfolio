## 6. A small shadow that caused big trouble

So far, most of the work was about removing things: fewer fragments, fewer blades, less work where it didn't matter. This part
goes in the opposite direction.

The grass still looked a bit flat.

Not wrong, just... a bit detached. Blades were moving nicely, density felt right, but they didn't quite sit in the world.
They floated instead of belonging to the ground.

The fix was obvious: a bit of ambient occlusion.

Nothing fancy. Just a subtle darkening near the base of each blade to hint at contact with the ground and give the field
some depth. I had done this before, so I added it almost automatically. This isn't screen-space AO or anything global, it's a
tiny, per-blade shading term.

After this addition, I immediately ran into a familiar problem.

In earlier versions, I was applying AO along the entire blade. Thin geometry plus dark shading at a distance is a great recipe
for aliasing, especially once blades start collapsing to sub-pixel sizes. Far blades started to shimmer. The grass gained depth,
but lost stability.

<br />

This time I treated it more carefully.

Instead of occluding the whole blade, I limited AO to a small region near the base. The effect fades out quickly as you
move up the blade, leaving the tip completely untouched. I also clamped both the radius and the intensity based on
distance, so farther grass isn't affected at all.

The result is deliberately understated.

Close to the player, blades feel grounded. Far away, the field stays clean and stable. No dark speckling, no crawling
edges, no extra noise competing with motion.

It didn't change performance in any meaningful way. That was never the goal. It simply removed a visual distraction I
had created for myself in the past.

As a side effect, it became a nice artistic control. By dialing the AO down even further, the grass shifts toward a more
anime-like look. Pushing it slightly brings it back toward something more grounded. Same system, different mood.

dial whenever I want.
