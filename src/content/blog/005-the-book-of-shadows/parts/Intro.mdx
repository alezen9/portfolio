For over a year, pretty much since [Revo](https://revo-realms.aleksandargjoreski.dev/)'s world had more in it than just the
player, every static shadow went through the same little ritual: change something in Blender, bake the shadows onto the terrain,
export them, pack them together with the other terrain textures, compress everything to KTX2, load it in the project and sample
the right texture in each object that needed it...quite the process.

And if an object had to shadow itself, like the Goku statue for example, it needed its own bake on its own texture, and the whole
dance started again.

None of it was hard on its own, and over time I even wrote a few scripts to take care of the packing and converting. It was just a
long process that made me hesitant to add or change things. I would look at a rock, think it would look better a couple of metres
to the left, remember what moving it meant, and leave it exactly where it was.

Additionally, apart from the player, there was essentially no plan for anything that moved, so I kept putting off adding dynamic
objects altogether. For a project I use as a sandbox, that was a constraint I was willing to accept, and for a long time I was happy
to live with it.

Perhaps it was time to finally switch gears :)

Like many other beginners, I too started with the built-in shadow map: a directional light, `castShadow`, `receiveShadow` and a
shadow camera following the player around. It's a great way to start, but as the world got bigger and the camera could rotate
freely, I found myself either losing quality or paying way too much for it, so baked shadows crept in pretty early on.

Along the way I also gave cascaded shadow maps a try, but I didn't manage to make them work well, most likely because of a skill
issue on my end, so back to baking it was, with a tiny shadow map just for the player. Later I got rid of even that and gave the
player an analytical shadow in post instead. It's a sphere after all, so the math was fairly straightforward.

Still, shadows stayed somewhere at the back of my head for a long time, and back in September I finally decided to open that
Pandora's box with a simple question: how do huge open worlds or big engines like Unreal deal with this?

I wanted to learn how shadows actually work, understand the internals and the techniques behind them, and then build
something that fits my project and holds up as the world grows. Dynamic shadows included, but most importantly no more baking the
same thing over and over again. Crucially, it had to have very small overhead from day one, not something to fix later.

Turns out cascades were just the middle step.

There was an even more advanced technique called ==virtual shadow mapping==, and it looked just complex enough to get me hooked,
so I jumped straight into it.

That's pretty much how this whole thing started :)

To actually understand it though, I had to go back to the basics first, and this article takes that same road: from the plain
shadow map I had been using since the beginning without ever looking inside, through the cascades that got the better of me, to
the tailored version of virtual shadow maps running in Revo today.
