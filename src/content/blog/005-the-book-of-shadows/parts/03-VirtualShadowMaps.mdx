## 3. Virtual shadow maps

After cascades, the research led me to what became my final destination on this journey: **virtual shadow maps**.

The more I dug into them, the more interesting they got, so I decided to try them in Revo, and I was stubborn enough to get
through it... one way or another :)

At its core, it's a bit of a mix of both previous chapters: depth stored from the sun's view and compared with what the camera sees,
like a classic shadow map, and more detail up close than far away, like cascades.

What's different is how the map is laid out, and how little of it actually lives in memory.

Instead of a few maps cut along the view in sequence, there's a stack of gigantic 16k ones (16,384 texels a side) nested around the camera,
called **levels**, and each level gets cut into small tiles called **pages**.

Of course, 16k maps compared to a bunch of 2k ones can sound a bit scary, but it's not that bad, just trust the process.

Every level has the same number of pages and texels (same 16k maps and same subdivision), what changes is how much ground they cover.

Level 0 is the smallest, right around the camera, so each page covers just a small patch and the shadow comes out sharp. Each
level after that covers twice as much ground with the same texels, so its pages get bigger and its shadows coarser.

Close things read the small levels, far things the big ones. A bit like the mipmaps of a texture, each one with half the detail
of the one before, except these don't shrink, they grow to cover more ground.

### Clipmaps

Those nested squares have a name, **clipmaps**, and the mipmap comparison wasn't random: a clipmap is a mipmap where every
level gets cut down to the same size around the viewer. The idea goes back to 1998, when a few people at Silicon Graphics wanted
to render the whole surface of the Earth as a single texture at one metre per texel. Its full mipmap would have taken about 11
petabytes, but only a tiny part of it is ever needed at once, so they kept just a fixed-size square of every level around the
viewer.

Virtual shadow maps use the same trick for directional lights like the sun, with the camera as the centre. Every level keeps the
same 16,384 texel square around it, seen from the sun, and since a pixel picks its level by its distance from the camera, the
result is rings of sharpness that get sharper the closer they are.

Each level is a grid of 128 x 128 pages, and each page has 128 x 128 texels, which is where those 16,384 come from. Revo has 7 of
them, from 0 to 6, and what changes from one to the next is just the amount of ground covered:

- **Level 0** &rarr; 64 metres across, about 4 millimetres per texel. Very sharp.
- **Level 3** &rarr; 512 metres across, about 3 centimetres. Still good.
- **Level 6** &rarr; 4 kilometres across, about 25 centimetres. Only for far away stuff.

So a page on level 0 is half a metre across, and one on level 6 about 32 metres.

The level comes from the distance to the render camera: up to 8 metres away it's level 0, from 8 to 16 metres level 1, from 16 to 32
level 2, and so on, each range twice as long as the one before, which in code is just:

```typescript
const LEVEL_0_MAX_DISTANCE_IN_METERS = 8;

// how many times 8m doubles to reach the point
const level = ceil(log2(distanceToCamera / LEVEL_0_MAX_DISTANCE_IN_METERS));
```

That doubling is on purpose. Each level's texels are twice as big as the previous one's, and something twice as far away looks
half as big on screen, so the two cancel out and the shadow looks about equally sharp everywhere.

With that, the far end of the view at 150 metres lands on level 5, at about 12.5 centimetres per texel. That's coarser than the
5.1 centimetres of the far cascade in the previous chapter, but it's also where shadows are smallest on screen. Right in front of
the camera, it's 4 millimetres instead of 1.3 centimetres, about three times sharper, and that's against a much narrower view
than Revo's.

Honestly, I didn't spend much time tuning how many levels there are. I tried a couple of variations, locked it at 7 and moved
on. Unreal goes with 17 by default, the widest one reaching about 40 kilometres, but Revo is a lot smaller than that.

As for cost, the levels themselves are almost free. Requested pages all go into the same fixed block of memory, whatever level
they come from, and if that ever runs out, some shadows simply won't show up. It's always the far ones that go first though, where
I'd barely notice, so I can live with that.

What the levels do change is how many pages get asked for. A finer level 0 would make everything close to the camera ask for 4
times as many pages, more to draw and that memory filling up faster, for details I wouldn't even notice. Fewer levels at the far end
would push distant ground onto finer pages than it needs, same problem. And more levels at the far end would cost next to nothing,
since nothing would ever ask for them past level 6.

Looking at it again while writing this, the far end of the view reads level 5, and level 6 is for things that ask for blurrier shadows on purpose, like grass
far away and my very basic volumetric light, so 7 is about where it stops paying off for me.

### Fugazi, fairy dust

Now, if all 7 levels really lived in memory, they'd take around 7 GiB, which is not something you want to hand to a browser tab.

Fortunately they don't, and that's what the virtual in the name is about.

A level is just a grid laid over the sun's view, so any point can work out which page and texel it lands in from where it sits
under the sun alone. Memory then only gets used for the pages something on screen actually needs.

That memory is a **pool**, a block of real memory set aside once and cut into **slots**, one page each. When a page is needed, it
grabs a free slot and gets drawn into it, while a **page table** keeps track of which page sits where. Once drawn, it stays there,
cached, until something in it changes, so the next frame can skip it entirely.

In Revo, that looks like this:

- **7 levels** &rarr; 16,384 x 16,384 texels each, about 1 GiB per level at 4 bytes per texel.
- **The pool** &rarr; 512 pages, about 32 MiB, far less than 7 GiB.

The size of the pool is up to me, and this one I actually spent a bit more time on.

Most of the time the camera asks for somewhere around 200 to 300 pages, but running fast through the map can push it past 600
on some frames. I also wanted a power of 2, for no real reason other than it feeling more organized that way, so 1024 felt a bit
wasteful and 512 ended up being a decent compromise.

So in the end, the camera decides which pages are needed, and the casters only care about which pages they cross. That's why the
off-screen tree isn't a special case anymore, and there's no light margin to tune like with cascades. It's just how the whole thing works.

Unfortunately, paging doesn't fix the usual shadow map problems. Each pixel still runs the same depth comparison as before, so
acne and Peter Panning can still show up and soft edges still need filtering. What paging changes is only which parts of the map
exist and get drawn.

### Static vs dynamic casters

Revo, at least for now, is mostly made of things that never move, like the trees, the statue and the campfire. Redrawing all of
them every time something changes would be a waste, and since every page can be cached on its own, the moving ones can get their
own layer without disturbing the rest.

There could be more layers, but I went with the two classic ones: one for static casters and one for moving ones. Both go through
pretty much the same pipeline, they just follow different rules about when their pages need drawing again. Easier said than done,
but it worked out in the end.

Every caster gets registered with its layer:

```typescript
// static by default, drawn once and cached
graphics.vsmPass.registerCaster(campfire);

// alpha tested, the canopy texture decides which texels cast
graphics.vsmPass.registerCaster(canopyBatch, {
  opacityNode: texture(assets.resources.pineTreeDiffuse).a,
});

// the player, moved by the cpu
graphics.vsmPass.registerCaster(this.mesh, { type: "dynamic" });

// the flag, simulated on the gpu, so the shadow reads the same positions
graphics.vsmPass.registerCaster(flag, {
  type: "dynamic",
  positionNode: particlePosition.add(vec3(this.origin)),
});
```

A static page lives in the 512 slot pool from earlier, gets drawn once and stays in its slot for as long as it's needed. Walking
around only draws the pages the camera hasn't needed before, and the coarse levels barely change, since each of their pages covers
so much ground. The trees, the rocks and the statues don't get drawn again just because time passed.

The bake became a cache :)

A dynamic page goes into a second, much smaller pool of 64 slots, and gets drawn again every frame. A moving thing only ever
touches its own pages, never the static cache underneath.

That's the whole point of keeping the two apart. The ball rolling through a forest would otherwise invalidate every tree page it
touches, every frame, and the cache would be gone right where the player is. Instead, the static depth stays where it is and the
ball's shadow follows it on its own. Shikamaru would be proud :)

64 doesn't sound like much, but the ball only ever uses between 5 and 15 of them. I started with 400 and cut it down, which took
the pool from 25 MiB to 4 MiB.

### Dissecting a frame

With a classic map or cascades, the sun draws first and the camera reads the shadow while it shades, but here the camera has
to go first, since it's the one deciding which pages are needed.

That's how Unreal Engine 5 does it, and Blender's EEVEE renders its shadows the same way.

Unreal's lighting is deferred by default, meaning the geometry gets drawn first, without any light, and the lights get applied on
top of it afterwards. So by the time shadows are needed, the depth is already there, and Unreal can mark the pages from it and
draw them right away.

Then, from every point visible on screen, it shoots a few rays toward the light, eight on its highest shadow settings.

It might sound like ray tracing, and in a way it is, just a tiny piece of it. A path tracer shoots rays from the camera through
every pixel and lets them bounce around the scene, picking up light and colour along the way, and at each bounce it also fires
one ray straight at the light to check whether anything is in the way. SMRT keeps only that last one: no bounces, no colour, just
"can I reach the sun from here?", starting from the point the camera already found.

It never touches the actual geometry either. Instead, a few points along each ray get checked against the pages, and how many of
the rays end up blocked is what softens the edge. Hence the name, SMRT (Shadow Map Ray Tracing).

Three.js, on the other hand, is a forward renderer. Every material shades itself with all of its lights in one go, shadows
included, so the shadow has to exist before the camera draws anything.

The common fix in forward renderers is a depth pre-pass, drawing the whole scene once just for its depth and then again to shade it.

For me that would mean drawing everything twice, a whole field of GPU grass included...so I went a different direction.

First things first, the scene gets rendered **once** from the main camera, with the real materials, writing 4 outputs into
a single render target (MRT), which get used along the way and at the end to put the final frame together:

- **Colour, all lights** &rarr; the scene lit by every light in it, ambient included, just no shadows.
- **Colour, sun only** &rarr; the same, but with only the sun contributing, no ambient. It tells the last pass how much light
  to subtract from the shadowed pixels of the all-lights colour.
- **Shadow softness** &rarr; a bit of extra control, so I can opt in for softer, blurrier shadows on foliage like the grass,
  because it simply looks much better and creates less aliasing.
- **Depth** &rarr; classic depth, nothing more, nothing less.

Then a compute pass reads that depth and, from it, rebuilds each pixel's world position. That position then gets projected into
the sun's view, as if the sun were the camera, to find where it lands on the shadow map.

In that same pass, that position's distance from the camera picks the level, like in the clipmaps earlier, and the page it lands
in at that level gets marked as requested.

A few more small compute passes then give each requested page a slot in the pool, unless it already has one from an earlier frame.

After that, another compute pass draws the casters into the pages that just got a slot, from the sun. Only depth gets written: for
each of the page's 128 x 128 texels, how close to the sun the nearest surface is. Together, the slots now hold the shadow map, just
in pieces.

Finally, a regular fullscreen pass goes over every pixel, finds its page the same way the requests did and reads the depth stored
there. If that depth is closer to the sun than the pixel's own position, something stands between them, so the pixel is in shadow
and its sun-only colour gets subtracted from the all-lights one.

The ambient light stays, which is why shade gets darker but never black.

### Page requests

The request pass is the first thing that runs once the scene has been drawn, since it needs that frame's depth. Right before it, a
tiny pass clears all the bits, and from there everything happens in the request pass itself. It doesn't run on every frame though,
only when something changes what the camera could be asking for: the camera moving or turning, the sun moving, or a caster being
added, moved, or removed.

Every page of every level gets a single bit, all packed into one buffer, 32 bits per number. That's 7 levels of 16,384 pages,
about 115,000 bits, only 14 KiB. A 1 means the page is requested, a 0 means nobody asked for it.

For every point visible on screen, the request pass picks its level and works out which page of that level it falls in, as a
coordinate on the level's page grid in the sun's view.

A point close to the edge of its page needs a bit more though. The shadow filter later reads a few texels around it to soften the edge,
and some of those can fall on the neighbouring page. If nobody requested that one, it has no depth in it, and the shadow breaks
right at the border. So the pass also requests every page within 3 texels of the point, which most of the time is just the same
page anyway.

For every requested page, the pass already knows its level and its column and row in that level's grid, and it packs all three
into a single number, the page's **key**: the level times the 16,384 pages of a level, plus its row times 128, plus its column.
That number is exactly where the page's bit sits in the buffer.

Let's consider a pixel showing something about 5 metres from the camera:

```text
level     0, since it's closer than 8 metres
page      column 70, row 3 in level 0's grid
key       level x 16,384 + row x 128 + column = 454
            0   x 16,384 +  3  x 128 +   70   = 454
number    454 / 32 = 14, the 15th number in the buffer
bit       454 - 14 x 32 = 6, the 7th bit inside it
```

Setting that bit to 1 is all it takes to request the page. In code, `key.div(32)` gives the number and `key.mod(32)` the bit.

In the sun's view the page grid goes on forever, but a level only covers the 128 x 128 pages around the camera, its **clipmap window**.
A page outside it can't be requested, since its key would wrap around onto a different page.

And since thousands of pixels want the same few pages, and GPUs really don't like many threads writing to the same address at
once, the pass reads the bit first and only writes it when it isn't set yet. For each of those pages, that looks like this:

```typescript
const key = getPageKey(level, pageCoordinate);
const word = requestBits.element(key.div(32));
const bit = uint(1).shiftLeft(key.mod(32)).toVar();
const isInWindow = isPageInWindow(pageCoordinate, windowCenter);
const isBitClear = atomicLoad(word).bitAnd(bit).equal(0);
const isNewRequest = isInWindow.and(isBitClear);
If(isNewRequest, () => {
  atomicOr(word, bit);
});
```

### The page pool

The request bits only say which pages are needed right now, but they get cleared and rebuilt every time the request pass runs.
Static pages on the other hand stay cached in their pool, so they don't get drawn again for nothing, and to keep track of what's
where in that pool I use two storage buffers.

The first one is the **page table**. Despite the name, it's just a flat buffer with an entry for every page that could exist,
114,688 in total, whether the page is in the pool or not. If page 454 sits in slot 17, entry 454 says 17.

The second one is the **slot metadata**, with an entry for each of the 512 slots. Entry 17 knows four things about whatever sits
in slot 17:

- **Page key** &rarr; which page sits in the slot, 454 in this case.
- **Page tag** &rarr; a second check that the slot holds the right page, since as the camera moves, two different places can end
  up with the same key. It'll make more sense in a few paragraphs.
- **Last requested frame** &rarr; say 1200. If the requests change at frame 1260 and page 454 isn't among them, it stays at 1200,
  and slot 17 is up for grabs.
- **Generation** &rarr; the counter's value when page 454 was drawn, say 5. The depth is up to date as long as the counter is
  still 5.

Every slot compares its generation against a single counter the pool keeps, which makes it easy to invalidate either the whole
pool or just a few pages. When the sun moves, every page goes stale at once, so instead of going through all 512 slots I simply
bump the counter and none of them match anymore. It's a known trick, also used outside of graphics where a shared version number
invalidates a whole group at once when it goes up.

When a still object moves, only the pages under its old and new position go stale, and bumping the counter would throw away every
other page too. So only those get their copy set to 0, which the counter never takes, while the rest of the pool stays as it is.

Still objects that move are also where my favourite bug came from. Past the edge of the map there's a large flat terrain plane
that follows the player, so there's always ground under your feet. A flat plane can't cast a shadow on anything, but I had
registered it as a static caster. So every frame it moved, and every frame the cache dutifully threw away every page it touched,
which was basically all of them. The system was doing exactly what I asked it to do.

All the timings in this chapter come from my MacBook with an M2 Pro, on a scripted walk through the map. On that walk, not
registering the plane took the average frame from about 9.8 to about 7.15 ms, and the worst frames from 24 ms to about 8.

> There are 2 hard problems in computer science: naming things, cache invalidation and off-by-one errors.

This time it was the second one :)

In the page table the index is the page key, and in the slot metadata it's the slot number, so neither has to store it. Both get
created once on the CPU and never grow:

```typescript
const VSM_PAGE_GRID_SIZE = 128;
const VSM_PAGES_PER_LEVEL = VSM_PAGE_GRID_SIZE ** 2;
const VSM_LEVEL_COUNT = 7;
const VSM_PAGE_COUNT = VSM_PAGES_PER_LEVEL * VSM_LEVEL_COUNT;
const VSM_POOL_CAPACITY = 512;
// 0 is a valid page key, so empty slots hold the largest uint instead
const VSM_INVALID_PAGE_KEY = 0xffffffff;

const pageTable = new StorageBufferAttribute(
  new Uint32Array(VSM_PAGE_COUNT * 4),
  4,
);

const initialSlotMetadata = new Uint32Array(VSM_POOL_CAPACITY * 4);
for (let slot = 0; slot < VSM_POOL_CAPACITY; slot++)
  initialSlotMetadata[slot * 4] = VSM_INVALID_PAGE_KEY;
const slotMetadata = new StorageBufferAttribute(initialSlotMetadata, 4);
```

On the GPU, every entry is read as a `uvec4`:

```typescript
const pageTableEntry = uvec4(
  staticSlot.add(1), // static pool slot + 1, so 0 can mean no slot
  frame, // a dynamic caster touched this page, if equal to the current frame
  dynamicSlot, // dynamic pool slot, only valid while the frame above is current
  frame, // last frame this page was on screen
);

const slotMetadataEntry = uvec4(
  pageKey, // the page this slot holds
  pageTag, // its exact coordinates, since wrapped pages share a key
  frame, // last frame it was requested, an older one makes the slot reusable
  pageGeneration, // copy of the pool's counter, stale once the two differ
);
```

Everything so far only uses the first number of a page table entry. The other three are for the dynamic pool.

Whenever the requests change, a few small compute passes go through every requested page. Page 454, for example, can end up in one
of three ways:

- **It already has a slot** &rarr; entry 454 says slot 17, and slot 17 still says 454 with the current generation. The page keeps
  its depth, and slot 17 gets the current frame number.
- **It has no slot** &rarr; it goes on a list of missing pages. Any slot that didn't get the current frame number is free to take,
  empty ones first and then the ones holding pages nobody asked for. Once page 454 gets one, both buffers are updated.
- **No free slot is left** &rarr; it stays missing, and so does that bit of shadow.

Finer levels get first pick of the free slots, since close detail is what you'd notice. When the pool runs out, it's the pages far
away that go without, and I'd rather lose a bit of distant shadow than the one under your feet :)

Blender and Unreal hit the same wall when their pools run out: Blender shows a "shadow buffer full" error, and Unreal's docs warn that going over the limit
corrupts the shadows, checkerboard patterns included.

The page table has one catch though. Each level only covers the 128 x 128 pages around the camera, and as the camera moves, the
pages coming in on one side reuse the entries of the ones that just left on the other. On level 0, for example, entry 454 can
stand for a page right here or one 64 metres away. That's what the page tag is for: a lookup only counts if the tag matches too.

<br />

### Drawing pages in compute

Now the pages need their depth, and this is the part where I diverged the most from Unreal.

The job itself is simple to say: for every page that just got a slot, take the casters standing in its column of sunlight and
write, for each of its 128 x 128 texels, how close to the sun the nearest one is.

I started with the obvious approach: a regular render pass from the sun into one big depth texture, an atlas with every slot as a
128 x 128 tile in it, and each caster drawn only into the pages it touched. It worked, but WebGPU made it awkward in three ways:

- **No multi draw indirect** &rarr; every caster geometry was its own draw call, sent from the CPU. Packing the casters into
  clusters later turned them into a single indirect draw, so that one I got around.
- **No viewport per page within a draw** &rarr; with all the pages sharing a single draw, a triangle drawn into one tile can't be
  cut at the tile's edges, so a big one spills over the
  neighbouring tiles, and every texel outside its page has to be thrown away in the fragment shader. Wasted work, and when I tried
  finer levels, the pine canopy turned it into a proper spike.
- **No skipping a render pass** &rarr; which pages just got a slot is a number only the GPU knows, and the CPU could only find out
  by reading it back, a frame late. So whenever the pool changed, the pass ran anyway, loading and storing 48 MB of depth even with
  nothing new to draw.

The last one I couldn't get around, so the pages ended up being filled by a compute shader instead, rasterizing the triangles by
hand. The GPU builds its own list of work, and each item only ever writes the texels of its own page, so nothing spills over.

Unreal isn't far from this either. Nanite rasterizes its small triangles in compute and leaves the big ones to the hardware, and
its docs say plainly that geometry without Nanite is much more expensive to draw into pages. Without Nanite, compute does it all
here.

First, the casters get split into small **clusters** of 64 triangles, with all their vertices in one shared buffer. Each cluster
gets a bounding box in the sun's view, and for every page that needs drawing, the clusters overlapping it become work items, all
generated on the GPU: "draw cluster 12 into page 305". The off-screen tree is in there too, because only the sun's view matters in
this step.

The clusters also fixed one of the weirdest bugs of the whole thing. At some point I built a stress rig that filled the world with
casters of every kind, and in it a sphere was casting a cube shaped shadow. Seeing that is a special kind of confusing :) The
objects were packed into a `BatchedMesh`, and every instance was drawing the whole packed geometry, so they all cast each other's
shapes. The pines had the same problem, just less obvious. Clusters only ever point at the triangles of their own instance, so it
went away with them.

Each work item gets a group of 64 threads, one per triangle. First, every thread projects the corners of its triangle into the
page's texel grid and leaves them in shared memory. Then the group goes through the triangles one at a time, splitting the texels
of each triangle's bounding box between the threads.

For each texel, three **edge functions** say whether its centre is inside the triangle. An edge function is just the signed area
of the triangle formed by an edge and the point: positive on one side, negative on the other. Divided by the area of the whole
triangle, the three of them become barycentric weights, how much each corner contributes to that texel, and the same weights
interpolate the depth:

```typescript
const getEdge = (from: Node<"vec2">, to: Node<"vec2">, point: Node<"vec2">) => {
  const edge = to.sub(from);
  const offset = point.sub(from);
  return edge.x.mul(offset.y).sub(edge.y.mul(offset.x));
};

// simplified, for one texel of one triangle
const center = vec2(x, y).add(0.5);
const firstWeight = getEdge(second.xy, third.xy, center).div(area);
const secondWeight = getEdge(third.xy, first.xy, center).div(area);
const thirdWeight = getEdge(first.xy, second.xy, center).div(area);
const smallestWeight = firstWeight.min(secondWeight).min(thirdWeight);
If(smallestWeight.greaterThanEqual(0), () => {
  const texelDepth = first.z
    .mul(firstWeight)
    .add(second.z.mul(secondWeight))
    .add(third.z.mul(thirdWeight));
  const depthIndex = pageBase.add(y.mul(VSM_PAGE_TEXELS)).add(x);
  atomicMin(depth.element(depthIndex), uint(texelDepth.mul(DEPTH_SCALE)));
});
```

Several triangles can land on the same texel and GPU threads finish in any order, so each texel keeps the closest value with an
atomic min. Alpha tested casters like the pine canopy also interpolate their uvs with those weights and check the texture's opacity
before writing.

What gets stored is actually a height rather than a distance. The sun is an orthographic light above the world, so along each ray,
the higher point is the one it meets first, which is all the comparison needs.

As for the gain: with compute, an empty list of work items just means the shader has nothing to do. There's no depth target to
load and store anymore, one pass less, and it runs in the same compute call as the rest of the shadow work.

On the scripted walk, the average frame went from about 7.15 to about 7 ms, and the worst ones from about 8.1 to about 7.5.

It also means one rasterizer for every kind of caster: static meshes, `BatchedMesh` instances, things the CPU moves around and
things whose shape only exists on the GPU. Rigid moving casters cost the same as with the render pass. Only thousands of instances
positioned on the GPU came out slower, by about a quarter of a millisecond, and Revo doesn't get anywhere near that many.

{/* media, screenshot: the "Static depth" debug view (Postprocessing > View > Static depth), darker is higher. Capture near the Goku statue or a group of pines, so recognisable shapes show up in the stored heights, ideally with the same camera as the "Pages" screenshot. */}

Dynamic casters go through the same rasterizer, just every frame. Each of their clusters marks the requested pages it covers,
directly on the GPU, and only those pages get a dynamic slot and a fresh draw.

Those marks go into the three page table numbers I skipped earlier. Every frame, each page on screen gets the current frame number
written into its last number, and the clusters then write it into the second number of the on-screen pages they cover. Those pages
then get a dynamic slot, stored in the third. A page has a moving shadow only when its second number matches the current frame, so
old marks simply stop counting and nothing ever needs clearing.

Moving things come in two flavours. Things moved from the CPU, like the player, are caught when their world matrix changes. Things
whose shape only exists on the GPU, like the cloth of the flag, pass a `positionNode`, so the compute raster reads the same
positions the simulation wrote, and nothing has to come back to the CPU.

Unreal handles that case the other way around: a material that moves its vertices on the GPU, like foliage in the wind,
invalidates its cached pages every frame. Here it's simply a dynamic caster, and the static cache never notices.

Moving shadows aren't free though. A static page gets drawn once, a dynamic one gets cleared and drawn again every single frame,
all 128 x 128 texels of it. The ball covers a handful of pages, the flag about 20, and the flag alone costs around 0.2 to 0.3 ms.
A long static shadow is nearly free, a big moving one is not.

<br />

### Receiving the shadow

On the receiving side, it all comes down to the first and the last step of the frame.

The sun-only colour comes from the lit materials, like the terrain, the trees and the statues. They extend Three's own
`MeshLambertNodeMaterial` and `MeshStandardNodeMaterial`, and swap in a lighting model that measures how much light the sun adds,
by comparing the direct light before and after the sun's turn:

```typescript
// simplified, only the sun goes through here
direct(lightData: DirectLight, builder: LightingBuilder) {
  const { reflectedLight } = lightData;
  const diffuseBefore = vec3(reflectedLight.directDiffuse).toVar();
  super.direct(lightData, builder);
  const diffuseGain = vec3(reflectedLight.directDiffuse).sub(diffuseBefore);
  this.directSun.addAssign(diffuseGain);
}
```

Getting that into the render target is the part I didn't know about. Every node material has an `mrtNode`, and whatever outputs it
lists get merged into the outputs of the pass it's drawn in:

```typescript
this.mrtNode = mrt({ directSun: vec4(lightingModel.directSun, 1) });
```

The scene pass declares the sun-only output with a default of zero, so any material that doesn't fill it simply has no sun to take
away.

In the last pass, every point reads its static page and, if there's one, its dynamic page too, and the two results get multiplied,
so being in either shadow is enough.

{/* media, two screenshots side by side, same camera: the "Shadow" debug view (white lit, black shadow, grey penumbra) and the final frame. Pick a spot with a sharp contact shadow and a wide penumbra in view, like the statue on its pedestal. */}

The filtering is the PCSS from the first chapter: look around the point for blockers, then spread 8 samples around it, rotated a
little differently on every pixel, and wider the further away the blockers are. A sample crossing a page border looks up the neighbouring page, which is why requests have that margin,
and when the penumbra is tiny, a cheap 3 x 3 filter takes over from the full search.

My first version of it added 1.25 ms on its own. Two small changes brought it down to about 0.33: looking up the centre page once
and sharing it between all the samples, instead of every sample looking it up again, and only sampling the moving shadows where
there actually are any.

This is the step where Unreal shoots its rays toward the light instead, but more on that later.

The bias from the first chapter is still there too. Each receiver forgives 3 texels of its own level, plus a bit more on slopes, so
just like with cascades it grows with the texel size. A caster can also push itself a little further from the sun with its own
`depthBias`, in metres. The Goku statue uses 20 centimetres.

<br />

### Grass

Grass was one of the main reasons I wanted real shadows in the first place, and it got its own treatment on both sides.

As a caster, I did try it. Every blade drawn into the dynamic pages, moving with the wind. It looked quite bad, even with soft
shadows: a field of noisy speckles crawling around with every gust. Maybe it needed much, much softer shadows, I'm not sure.

{/* media, screenshot: the old experiment with every blade casting. It is in git at def874e^, src/content/blog/006-paging-my-shadows/assets/grass-shaded-earlier.png, convert it to webp first (it is 7.6 MB). */}

It was also breaking my performance budget. On my machine the frame went from about 5 milliseconds of GPU time to about 7 with
every blade casting, and still around 6 after a performance pass. My hard limit is to stay under 6, ideally under 5, at 120 fps,
and that was for something that looked worse. So grass casting got ditched.

I also looked at how others handle details that small, and found Bend Studio's write-up on **screen space shadows**:
marching through the depth buffer toward the sun to pick up what the shadow map misses. They used it in _Days Gone_, and they even [published the code](https://www.bendstudio.com/blog/inside-bend-screen-space-shadows/). I've never
played it myself though, zombies aren't really my kind of thing.

So I tried that as well. It cost about 0.8 ms, and the result was mush: soft dark smudges that didn't read as blade shadows at
all. Removed too.

As a receiver though, it stayed, and it works great.

Grass shades itself instead of going through the regular materials, but writes the same direct sun target, so the same resolve
handles it. It also writes a softness value, and so do the flowers, the pine canopy and the ground under the grass. These **soft
receivers** read coarser levels than their distance would pick, dithered between two neighbouring ones, so the shadow of a tree
falls across the blades as a smooth gradient instead of breaking into sharp fragments.

Thin, noisy surfaces asking for coarser pages also means fewer pages overall, so it helps the pool too.

Out past the edge of the map, where there's nothing but grass, terrain and the ball, the grass was also running its full shadow
filter on pages with nothing drawn in them, which can only ever say "lit". Now every page remembers whether anything was drawn into
it, and the filter skips the ones where nothing was, about 0.3 ms less out there.

And with 4x MSAA, a pixel at the edge of a blade can hold both the blade and the ground behind it. For those, the resolve shades
both surfaces and mixes them by how much of the pixel each one covers, otherwise the shadow ends up on the wrong one.

### Comparison is the thief of joy

Looking back at the whole thing, most of my setup follows what Unreal does: the clipmaps, pages cached between frames, moving
things kept apart from the still ones, and a moving sun throwing the whole cache away, since every page is tied to the sun's
direction.

There is some divergence though, sometimes because WebGPU didn't have what I needed, like with drawing the pages in compute,
and other times it was simply complexity I didn't need.

The soft edges went their own way too. Instead of Unreal's rays, I went with filtering, using the PCSS from the first chapter. Out
of curiosity I did try a quick version of the rays, and the shadows came out slightly sharper, but honestly, to me the filtered
ones looked a bit better, and the rays costed a little more too. Not by much, but enough for me to drop that small detour and stay
with my original version.

On the complexity front, Unreal does A LOT, and most of it is far from trivial. Very fascinating if you ask me, but bringing it
in Revo would mostly mean more things to dig into, understand and eventually maintain.

Unreal's virtual shadow maps also cover point and spot lights, each with its own set of pages, while Revo only has the sun. And
Nanite doesn't stop at clusters: it breaks every mesh into a hierarchy of them at different levels of detail, and swaps them on
the fly depending on the view.
Here in Revo, just plain clusters of 64 triangles, it does the job and pretty well I'd say.

I might still get to some of that stuff one day, just for the sake of learning new things because it's fun, but for now I just
wanted (nice and performant) shadows :)

<br />

### What it costs

Speaking of performant: on my M2 Pro, walking through the map, the whole shadow system costs about 2 ms of GPU time. Roughly
half of that is the resolve, which runs every frame, and the other half is the page work, which mostly stops as soon as nothing
moves.

The real catch is the sun. When it moves, the generation goes up, every page becomes stale and the whole cache gets drawn again.
Epic's own write-up on shadows in Fortnite says the same: when the light direction changes, all the cached data has to go, because
the pages themselves land in different places. Revo's sun doesn't move unless I move it, so that one was an easy trade to make.

{/* media, video (optional): the "Page heat" debug view while dragging "Sun position X" in the debug panel (Light folder), every page turning red at once on each change. */}

And even trimmed down like this, it's still a lot of moving parts: requests, slots, tags, generations, clusters and two pools,
all living on the GPU, where a bug mostly shows up as a shadow in the wrong place, or no shadow at all.

But once the sun's depth is sitting there in pages, cached and cheap to read, it can do more than darken things. If a point in the
air can look up whether the sun reaches it, the light itself can start to show up between the trees. That's the next chapter.
