## 1. Classic shadow maps

In my head, a shadow map was a normal texture with shadows in it. Maybe not the best way to describe it though, I thought it was
something that somehow the scene sampled, and used that value to darken its colours. A bit like laying an image over the world
from above.

I hadn't thought much further than that, and Three.js hadn't really given me a reason to. Enable `shadowMap` on the renderer,
let the light cast shadows, set `castShadow` and `receiveShadow` on the right meshes, and there you go.

The shadow map is indeed a texture yes, but what it actually holds is **depth**, seen from the light.

That was the first thing I had to get my head around. The texture records how far the light gets before hitting something, and
the scene uses that information to work out whether the light can reach each point we're looking at.

Simplified quite a bit, the whole thing comes down to three steps:

1. Before the frame, render the casters from the light's point of view, keeping only their depth. That texture is the
   **shadow map**.
2. Render the scene from the main camera as usual.
3. During step 2, while each point works out its lighting, project it into the light's view to find where it lands on the shadow
   map and how far it is from the light (its `z`). If it's farther than the stored depth, something got in the way and that light
   doesn't reach it, otherwise it's lit.

The result only dims that one light's contribution, not the whole colour, which is also where my baked shadows went wrong.

Putting the player under a tree in Revo made this easier for me to picture. Here's the scene seen from the sun, keeping only

<br />

Written in TSL, that comparison takes just a few lines. This is a simplified version of what the built-in Three.js shadows do
with an orthographic shadow camera:

```typescript
const lightSpacePosition = lightShadowMatrix(light).mul(vec4(positionWorld, 1));
const shadowUv = vec2(lightSpacePosition.x, float(1).sub(lightSpacePosition.y));
const shadowMapDepth = texture(shadowMap, shadowUv).r;
const isLit = lightSpacePosition.z.lessThanEqual(shadowMapDepth.add(bias));
```

The sun is so far away that its rays reach us practically parallel, so its shadow camera is orthographic. That's also why there's
no divide by `w` in the code: an orthographic projection always leaves it at 1. Other lights aren't that simple, though. A spot
light renders its shadow through a perspective camera, which does need the divide, and a point light renders a cube map, one view
for each direction.

The `bias` in the last line gives the comparison a little tolerance. Without it, a surface can start shadowing itself.

<br />

### Shadow acne and Peter Panning

With limited resolution and depth precision, a surface can end up just behind the depth stored for itself. The comparison
decides it's in shadow, and ugly dark stripes appear across it: **shadow acne**. The bias gives it a little room, so the two
depths no longer have to match quite so closely.

Too much room, though, and the shadow starts detaching from the object. Even the ground right under its base passes the comparison
as lit, so the object looks like it's floating. That's **Peter Panning**, named after Peter Pan, whose shadow came loose and had
to be sewn back on :)

### Filtering techniques

Even with the bias set, a single comparison still gives a hard yes or no. That leaves a razor-sharp edge, and a blocky one once
the texels get big.

I wanted the tree shadows to soften as they spread across the ground, while keeping the contact with each object sharp.

Real lights have a size, which is where that softness comes from, but a basic shadow map knows nothing about it, so the softness
has to be faked somehow.

The filtering options started making more sense once I looked at what each one changed about that same comparison:

- **Basic** &rarr; one comparison per pixel, so each pixel is either fully lit or fully in shadow. The cheapest option, but the
  edges are sharp and get blocky when texels are big.
- **PCF** (percentage-closer filtering) &rarr; compares a few texels around the pixel and averages the results, so the edge fades
  from lit to shadow instead of snapping. The softness is the same everywhere, near the caster or far from it.
- **PCSS** (percentage-closer soft shadows) &rarr; PCF with a variable filter size. It first checks how far the caster is from the surface
  its shadow falls on, then uses a small filter when it's close and a wider one when it's far, so the shadow is sharp at the contact
  point and softer further away.
- **VSM** (variance shadow maps, same acronym as virtual shadow maps but unrelated) &rarr; stores the depth and the depth squared
  in two channels, and blurs both. Blurring depth alone would only mix the canopy and the ground into a made-up depth in between,
  but with both, each texel knows the average depth around it and how much it varies, which is enough for a pixel to estimate how
  much of that area sits between it and the light. Soft edges come cheap, although light can leak where casters at very different
  heights overlap.

### Size vs quality

Filtering only changes how the edges look, though. What actually made me give up on the built-in shadow map back then was how
much of the world a single map had to cover.

Revo's camera sees 150 metres ahead and over 200 metres across at the far end. A 4096 map stretched over that gives each texel
around 6 centimetres of ground, fine for a distant tree but pretty blocky right under the player. I could keep the map tight
around the player, but then I'd completely lose the shadows further out.

A bigger texture gets expensive quickly. At 32 bits per texel, 4096 &times; 4096 is 64 MiB, and 8192 is already 256 MiB,
for a single light. Also, every time the map updates, the casters inside its camera have to be drawn into it again.

So for a while I used a hybrid approach: everything but the player was static, so it got baked, which let me shrink the
real-time map to a tiny 64 by 64 over just 2 by 2 metres around the player (yep, the ball is a whole metre across, it just
doesn't look like it). That's about 3 centimetres per texel for almost nothing, since all it had to hold was one ball.

It worked well, but only as long as nothing else moved. A new object or a different sun meant another bake, and anything else
that moved had no shadow at all.

On paper, cascaded shadow maps are the go-to answer for exactly this. But my curiosity, as usual, got the best of me, and I kept
digging past them...
