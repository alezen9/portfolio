## 1. Classic shadow maps

In a classic Three.js scene, getting shadows is mostly a matter of flipping a few switches: enable `shadowMap` on the renderer,
let the light cast them, set `castShadow` on the meshes that should cast a shadow and `receiveShadow` on the ones it should land on.
That's pretty much it.

What happens behind the scenes is fairly simple, at least conceptually, and honestly pretty fascinating:

1. Render the casters from the light's point of view, keeping only the depth: how far the light travelled before hitting
   something.
2. Render the scene from the main camera as usual.
3. Project each point on screen into the light's view, to find where it lands on the depth map and how far it is from the light
   (its `z`), then compare: if it's farther than the depth stored there, something got in the way and it's in shadow, otherwise
   it's lit.

To ground it with a real example, here's what happens when the player in Revo stands in the shadow of a tree. Step 1 renders the
scene from the sun's point of view, and that depth looks something like this:

==The image on the left is what each point compares itself against==: for every pixel, the sun's depth stored at the spot it lands
on. It's not the camera's depth, and it's not each point's own distance from the sun either, it's what the map says the sun hit
first along that ray. In the tree's shadow, the first thing the sun hits is the canopy, so every point on the player reads the
canopy's depth instead, which is closer to the sun than the player itself. That's why the tree's darker shape lands exactly where
its shadow is in the final frame. Out in the open, on the other hand, nothing stands between the grass and the sun, so the depth
stored there belongs to the grass itself: same value, nothing in the way, so it stays lit.

> All the screenshots in this chapter come from Revo, which already runs on the virtual shadow maps I'll get to later, but these
> concepts are the same as with a classic shadow map.

<br />

In a shader, that third step is only a few lines. A simplified version in TSL, close to what the built-in Three.js shadows do for
you, looks something like this:

```typescript
const lightClip = lightViewProjection.mul(vec4(positionWorld, 1));
const shadowUv = vec2(
  lightClip.x.mul(0.5).add(0.5),
  lightClip.y.mul(-0.5).add(0.5),
);
const receiverDepth = lightClip.z;
const storedDepth = texture(shadowMap, shadowUv).r;
const isLit = receiverDepth.lessThanEqual(storedDepth.add(bias));
```

Move the point into the light's view, read what's stored there, compare. That's the whole trick :) The `bias` is a small tolerance
on that comparison, and it's there because of shadow acne.

### Shadow acne and Peter Panning

**Shadow acne** comes from the map's resolution. Each texel of the map stores a single depth for a small patch of surface, so
points inside that patch compare themselves against a value that's slightly off from their own, and some of them end up thinking
they're shadowing themselves. That's where those ugly dark stripes come from. The bias forgives that small difference, but push it
too far and the shadow detaches from whatever casts it, so things look like they're floating. That one goes by "Peter Panning" :)

### Filtering techniques

> The sun is the easy case: its rays are parallel, so one orthographic map does the job. A spot light needs a perspective map
> shaped like its cone, and a point light needs six, one per cube face.<br />
> Real lights also have a size, which is what makes shadows sharp near the contact point and softer further away. A shadow map
> treats the light as a single point, so that softness always has to be faked.

<br />

Faking it is what shadow filtering is about. A single comparison is a hard yes or no, so on its own every shadow ends with a
razor-sharp edge, and a blocky one too once texels get big. The usual fix is **PCF** (percentage-closer filtering): compare a few
neighbours too and average the results. The results, not the depths, because averaging the canopy's depth with the grass' depth
would invent a surface that isn't really there. Revo uses PCSS from the list below: it widens the blur the further away the caster
is, which is why the shadow stays sharp at the contact point.

> There are quite a few ways to sample a shadow map, perhaps the most common ones are:
>
> - **Basic** &rarr; one comparison per pixel. Fastest, with hard edges, blocky at low resolution.
> - **PCF** (percentage-closer filtering) &rarr; several comparisons around the point, averaged. GPUs even have a comparison
>   sampler that blends four of them in a single fetch, so a small PCF comes almost for free.
> - **PCSS** (percentage-closer soft shadows) &rarr; PCF with a blur that widens the further away the blocker is, faking the soft
>   light from the note above.
> - **VSM** (variance shadow maps) &rarr; stores depth and depth squared, so the map itself can be blurred. Cheap soft edges, but
>   light can bleed through where shadows overlap.
>
> This VSM has nothing to do with the one this article is about. Same acronym, completely different thing :)

### Size vs quality

What really limits a single shadow map though is scale, and that one I already knew very well. A shadow map grows with the square
of its resolution, so a 4096 x 4096 map at 32 bits is 64 MiB, at 8192 it's already 256 MiB, and that's for a single light. On top
of that, every caster gets drawn a second time into it, every frame if anything moves.

And whatever resolution you pick gets spread over the whole area the map covers. A 4096 map over about 512 metres gives each texel
roughly 12.5 centimetres of ground, which is fine for a distant hill and pretty hopeless right under the player. So either you fit
the map tightly around the player and lose the hills, or you cover the whole world and everything nearby gets blurry and blocky.

That's exactly the trade I kept losing with the built-in shadows: one map can be wide or sharp, never both. What I needed was a
way to spend resolution only where it would actually be seen.
