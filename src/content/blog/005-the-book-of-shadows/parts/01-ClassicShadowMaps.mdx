## 1. Classic shadow maps

In a classic Three.js scene, getting shadows is mostly a matter of flipping a few switches: enable `shadowMap` on the renderer,
let the light cast them, set `castShadow` on the meshes that should cast a shadow and `receiveShadow` on the ones it should land
on.

That's pretty much it.

What happens behind the scenes is also fairly simple, at least conceptually, and pretty fascinating as well:

1. Render the casters from the light's point of view into a texture, keeping only the depth, which tells how far the light
   travelled before hitting something. That texture is the **shadow map**, whatever the light is, the sun or any other.
2. Render the scene from the main camera as usual.
3. Project each point on screen into the light's view, to find where it lands on the shadow map and how far it is from the light
   (its `z`), then compare: if it's farther than the depth the shadow map holds there, something got in the way and it's in shadow, otherwise
   it's lit.

To ground it with a real example, here's what happens when the player in Revo stands in the shadow of a tree. Step 1 renders the
scene from the sun's point of view, and that depth looks something like this:

The image on the left is what each pixel compares itself against. For every pixel, it shows the depth the shadow map holds where
that pixel lands, so what the sun hit first along that ray, **not** the main camera's depth.

In the tree's shadow, the first thing the sun hits is the canopy, so every point on the player reads the canopy's depth instead,
which is closer to the sun than the player itself.

That's why the tree's darker shape lands exactly where its shadow is in the final frame.

Out in the open, on the other hand, nothing stands between the ground and the sun, so the depth the shadow map holds there belongs to the ground
itself: same value, nothing in the way, so it stays lit.

> would look very similar, and the concepts are exactly the same.

<br />

In a shader, the comparison is only a few lines. Here's a simplified version in TSL, close to what the built-in Three.js shadows
do for you:

```typescript
// no divide by w needed since it's 1 for an orthographic light like the sun
const lightSpacePosition = lightShadowMatrix(light).mul(vec4(positionWorld, 1));
const shadowUv = vec2(lightSpacePosition.x, float(1).sub(lightSpacePosition.y));
const shadowMapDepth = texture(shadowMap, shadowUv).r;
const isLit = lightSpacePosition.z.lessThanEqual(shadowMapDepth.add(bias));
```

Move the position into the light's space, read the shadow map there, compare. That's the whole trick :)

The `bias` is a small tolerance on that comparison, and getting it right means balancing two of the most classic shadow map
problems.

<br />

### Shadow acne and Peter Panning

Shadow acne and Peter Panning sit at the two ends of the same dial, the `bias`, and the goal is a spot between them where
neither shows up.

The bias moves the depth read from the shadow map closer to or further from the light, only for the comparison, and that decides
how much room a surface gets before it counts as being behind something.

Move it too close and a surface ends up behind its own depth, even by a hair, so it shadows itself and gets covered in ugly dark
stripes. That's **shadow acne**.

Move it too far and even the base of an object touching the ground stops counting as a caster, so the shadow detaches from it and the object looks like it's floating. That's **Peter Panning**, named, as you've
probably guessed, after Peter Pan, whose shadow came loose and had to be sewn back on :)

### Filtering techniques

A single comparison is a hard yes or no, so on its own every shadow ends with a razor-sharp edge, and a blocky one too once texels
get big.

Real shadows don't look like that, because real lights have a size: right where an object touches the ground its shadow is
sharp, and the further it stretches, the softer its edge gets. A shadow map treats the light as a single point, so that softness
has to be faked, and that's what filtering is about.

The most common ways to do it:

- **Basic** &rarr; one comparison per pixel, so each pixel is either fully lit or fully in shadow. The cheapest option, but the
  edges are sharp and get blocky when texels are big.
- **PCF** (percentage-closer filtering) &rarr; compares a few texels around the pixel and averages the results, so the edge fades
  from lit to shadow instead of snapping. The softness is the same everywhere, near the caster or far from it.
- **PCSS** (percentage-closer soft shadows) &rarr; PCF with a variable size. It first checks how far the caster is from the surface
  its shadow falls on, then uses a small filter when it's close and a wider one when it's far, so the shadow is sharp at the contact
  point and softer further away.
- **VSM** (variance shadow maps) &rarr; saves the depth and the depth squared in two separate channels of the same shadow map.
  Blurring a plain depth wouldn't help, since at a shadow's edge it would only mix the canopy's depth with the ground's into a
  made-up depth in between, and the comparison would still be a hard yes or no. Blurring both values instead gives each texel the
  average depth of the area around it and how much the depths there vary. With those two, a pixel can estimate how much of that
  area sits between it and the light, and that becomes a soft shadow value. Soft edges come cheap this way, although some light can
  leak through where casters at very different heights overlap.

### Size vs quality

What really limits a single shadow map though is scale, and that one I already knew very well. A shadow map grows with the square
of its resolution, so a 4096 x 4096 map at 32 bits is 64 MiB, at 8192 it's already 256 MiB, and that's for a single light.

On top of that, every caster inside the shadow camera gets drawn a second time into it, every frame by default, whether anything
moved or not.

And whatever resolution you pick gets spread over the whole area the map covers. It doesn't have to be the whole world, since the
map can follow the camera and only cover what it sees, but with Revo's camera that's still 150 metres ahead and over 200 metres
across at the far end. A 4096 map stretched over that gives each texel around 6 centimetres of ground, fine for a distant hill and
pretty blocky right under the player.

So either you fit the map tightly around the player and lose the hills, or you stretch it over the whole view and everything nearby
gets blurry and blocky.

That's exactly the trade I kept losing with the built-in shadows: one map can be wide or sharp, never both.

Cascades, the next step, get around it by "simply" using more than one.
