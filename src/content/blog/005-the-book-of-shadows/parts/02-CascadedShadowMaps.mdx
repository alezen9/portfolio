## 2. Cascaded shadow maps

Cascades start from a pretty simple observation: not every part of the screen needs the same amount of detail.

Right in front of the camera, a single shadow texel can cover dozens of pixels, so it'd better be tiny. A hundred meters
away, that same texel shrinks to a pixel or less, and nobody would notice if it was ten times bigger.

So instead of one map stretched over everything, the view gets cut into a few slices by distance, and each slice gets its own map.

The first one covers just the few meters in front of the camera, the next one a bit more, and the last one everything far away,
all at the same resolution but with a very different amount of ground per texel.

It's what the big public engines have been shipping for years. Unity calls them shadow cascades, Godot splits the view in four by
default, and Unreal still has them, even though virtual shadow maps have been the default for new projects since Unreal Engine 5.

Three.js ships one too, `CSMShadowNode`, and that's the one I tried back then. It's been more than a year though, and honestly I
don't remember much about how it behaved in Revo, besides never getting it to work well, so what follows comes more from research
than from my own experience.

I still wanted it in here, since cascades are the step between a single shadow map and virtual shadow maps, and the picture
wouldn't be complete without them. It's also why this chapter comes without screenshots: Revo already runs on virtual shadow maps,
and I didn't want to bend the project just to showcase cascades.

<br />

Picking the right cascade in the shader is the easy part: take the pixel's depth from the camera, find the slice it falls in,
and run the same comparison from the previous chapter against that slice's map.

Deciding where to cut takes a bit more thought. Take, for example, a camera that sees from half a meter up to 150 meters, split
into three cascades with a 2048 map each. It's not Revo's camera, by the way: the range is the same, but I kept the view much
narrower so it better fits the drawings, that's all.

There are a few common ways to place the cuts:

- **Uniform** &rarr; slices of equal length, so the first one would run all the way to 50 meters and lose its detail.
- **Logarithmic** &rarr; each slice longer than the previous one by a fixed factor, so the first one would end at about 3 meters
  and the last one gets huge.
- **Practical** &rarr; halfway between the two, the Three.js default, which cuts at about 25 and 60 meters.

With the practical cuts, each slice gets a square map as wide as its longest diagonal, so the slice fits in it whichever way the
camera turns:

- **0.5 to 25 m** &rarr; a map of about 26 by 26 meters, roughly 1.3 centimeters per texel.
- **25 to 60 m** &rarr; about 41 by 41 meters, roughly 2 centimeters per texel.
- **60 to 150 m** &rarr; about 104 by 104 meters, around 5.1 centimeters per texel.

For that same view, a single 4096 map would give about 3.8 centimeters per texel everywhere. The three 2048 maps together take
48 MiB instead of 64, and still the near slice gets about three times the detail and the middle one almost twice as much. Only
the far slice loses a bit, right where the shadows are smallest on screen anyway.

Noice :)

My drawings here do cheat a little, but the idea remains the same: each map still has to hold its whole slice, whatever shape it is.

With the frustum this narrow, its longest diagonals run mostly along its length, while a real camera spreads much wider and the
width ends up deciding the size. They're also drawn from above, while the real maps face the sun (see drawing below), and the
ruler starts at 0 instead of half a meter to keep it readable.

### Shimmering, seams and off-screen casters

These maps now follow the camera around though, and that brings a few problems of its own.

The first one is shimmering, and it's easy to picture. Whenever the camera moves, the map moves with it and lands a fraction of a
texel away from where it was, so the edges get rasterized slightly differently every frame and shadows start crawling as you walk.
The fix is to move the map only in whole texel steps, so the texel grid stays put and the edges with it. Three.js does that by
snapping each cascade's center to its texel size.

Turning needs care too. A box fitted tightly around a slice would change size as the slice turns relative to the sun, and the
texel size would change with it. That's the reason for those longest diagonals earlier: the square keeps the same size through
the whole turn, at the cost of some unused space in its corners.

Then there are the seams, since the ground right at a cut suddenly switches to bigger texels, from about 2 to 5.1 centimeters at
60 meters in the example above, and that jump can show up as a line on the ground following the camera around, not exactly subtle.
`CSMShadowNode` has an optional `fade` that blends the two cascades across a small margin to hide it. That same jump matters for
the bias from the previous chapter too, since bigger texels need a bigger tolerance, so Three.js scales it up for each cascade.

The last one is easy to miss. The cascades are rendered from the sun's point of view, but each render still goes through an
orthographic camera, and a camera has to be placed somewhere. It only draws what sits between its near and far planes, so anything
standing further toward the sun than the camera itself never makes it into the map, even when its shadow falls right in view.

With a single shadow map, that place is the `DirectionalLight`'s own position: its shadow camera sits there, looks at the light's
target, and draws from 0.5 to 500 units ahead by default. Cascades place their own light cameras instead, one per slice.

Seen from the side, each one is the faded line touching its slice, centered on it across the sunlight and placed right at its
corner closest to the sun, the dot, so the whole slice sits in front of it.

That's enough to hold the slice, but not the tree. It stands behind the camera, out of view, yet its shadow still reaches into the
near slice, and it sits further toward the sun than that slice's faded camera, so the camera would never draw it.

Three.js solves this by pulling every cascade's light camera back toward the sun by the same `lightMargin`. 200 is the default,
which should be plenty for most scenes, but it's customizable.

With that, the tree sits in front of the near slice's camera and makes it into its map.

Unity seems to have taken a different approach to this problem: it keeps its light camera tight and flattens any caster
behind it onto the near plane, which it calls shadow pancaking.

It does sound appealing, but by then virtual shadow maps had already caught my attention, so... priorities. The name alone is
intriguing enough to tickle me from time to time, so I might get back to it someday, just out of sheer curiosity to see
what they're cooking (pun intended).

### Redrawing every frame

Even when they hold still, cascades pay the same price as the single map, just several times over. Each cascade is its own render
from the sun, and every caster that overlaps it gets drawn into it, so a tree sitting right at a cut ends up in both of them. With
`autoUpdate` on by default, that happens every single frame, whether anything moved or not.

In Revo, almost nothing that casts a shadow moves. The trees, the rocks that I don't have yet but plan to add, the statue and the
campfire stay right where they are, and the sun doesn't move unless I move it. Redrawing all of them every frame, sometimes into
more than one cascade, just to get the exact same depth back is a lot of work for nothing.

At least the work is predictable and stays the same across frames :)

One trick could be to refresh the far cascades less often, but the static stuff still gets redrawn from scratch, just not every
frame.

<br />

What I actually wanted was the opposite: draw the static stuff once and keep it, redraw only what changed, and instead of deciding
where the detail goes by distance, let the screen ask for exactly the parts of the map it needs.

That's pretty much what virtual shadow maps are about.
