## 2. Cascaded shadow maps

Cascades start from a pretty simple observation: not every part of the screen needs the same amount of detail.

Right in front of the camera, a single shadow texel can cover a good chunk of the screen, so it'd better be tiny. A hundred metres
away, that same texel shrinks to a pixel or less, and nobody would notice if it was ten times bigger.

So instead of one map stretched over everything, the view gets cut into a few slices by distance, and each slice gets its own map.
The first one covers just the few metres in front of the camera, the next one a bit more, and the last one everything far away.
Same resolution for each, but a very different amount of ground per texel.

It's what the big public engines have been shipping for years. Unity calls them shadow cascades, Godot splits the view in four by
default, and Unreal still has them, even though virtual shadow maps took over as the default for new projects in Unreal Engine 5.

Games on in-house engines are a different story: studios don't always share how their shadows work, so it's hard to say for sure
what each one uses, cascades, virtual shadow maps or something of their own.

Three.js ships one too: `CSMShadowNode`.

That's also the one I tried in Revo a while back, more than a year ago now. Honestly, I don't even remember what went wrong
anymore. Either I never managed to set it up properly, or it worked and was way too slow, but either way it didn't stick, and back
to baking it was.

I'm writing this with Revo already running on virtual shadow maps, so this chapter comes without screenshots. Getting real footage
of cascades would mean bending the project into a setup it was never built for, and they weren't the destination anyway: once the
initial research was done, paging was where I wanted to go.

Picking the right cascade in the shader is the easy part: take the point's distance from the camera, find the slice it falls in,
and run the same comparison from the previous chapter against that slice's map. The interesting part is deciding where to cut.

There are a few common ways to place the cuts:

- **Uniform** &rarr; equal lengths, so the near slice gets too long and loses its detail.
- **Logarithmic** &rarr; each slice longer by a fixed factor: sharp up close, huge far away.
- **Practical** &rarr; a bit of both, the Three.js default.

To put some numbers on it: Revo's camera sees from half a metre up to 150 metres, so three practical cascades cut at about 27 and
61 metres. With a 2048 map each, on a wide screen, that gives:

- **0.5 to 27 m** &rarr; about 45 metres across, roughly 2 centimetres per texel. Sharp.
- **27 to 61 m** &rarr; about 100 metres across, roughly 5 centimetres per texel. Still good.
- **61 to 150 m** &rarr; over 250 metres across, around 12 centimetres per texel. Not great up close, but more than fine for
  distant hills.

### Keeping cascades still

The catch is that these maps now follow the camera around, and that brings a few problems of its own.

The first one is shimmering. Every frame the map lands a fraction of a texel away from where it was, so the edges get rasterized
slightly differently and shadows start crawling as you walk. The fix is to move the map only in whole texel steps, so the grid,
and the edges with it, stay put. Three.js does that by snapping each cascade's centre to its texel size.

Rotation is the same problem in a different shape. Turn the camera and the slice changes shape with it, so a tightly fitted box
would change size every frame, and the texels with it. Instead, each cascade is sized for the worst case, the slice's longest
diagonal, and keeps that size whichever way the camera turns. A bit of resolution goes to waste in the corners, but it holds
still.

Then there are the seams. A point right at a cut jumps from 2 centimetre texels to 5 or so, and that jump can show up as a line on
the ground following the camera around. Not exactly subtle. `CSMShadowNode` has an optional `fade` that blends the two cascades
across a small margin to hide it. The bias needs the same care: bigger texels need a bigger tolerance, so Three.js scales it up
for each cascade too.

And finally, the one that's easy to miss. The cascades are fitted to what the camera sees, but a caster doesn't have to be in view
to throw a shadow into it.

Picture the player on the path and a pine tree just outside the frame, between the ball and the sun. The tree isn't in any slice,
so a box fitted tightly around the slices would cut it off, and its shadow would just vanish from the ground. So each cascade has
to stretch toward the sun, far enough to catch anything that could land in it. In Three.js that's `lightMargin`, which pulls each
cascade's light camera back by 200 units by default.

### Drawing it all again

Even when they hold still, cascades pay the same price as the single map, just more times. Each cascade is its own render from the
sun, and every caster that overlaps it gets drawn into it, so a tree close to the camera can easily end up in all three. With
`autoUpdate` on by default, that happens every single frame, whether anything moved or not.

And in Revo, almost nothing that casts a shadow moves. The trees, the rocks, the statues and the campfire stay right where they
are, and the sun doesn't move unless I move it. Redrawing all of them every frame, three times over, just to get the exact same
depth back, is a lot of work for nothing.

A common trick is to refresh the far cascades less often, but that only spreads the cost around.

What I actually wanted was the opposite: draw the static stuff once and keep it, redraw only what changed, and instead of deciding
where the detail goes by distance, let the screen ask for exactly the parts of the map it needs.

That's pretty much what virtual shadow maps are about.
