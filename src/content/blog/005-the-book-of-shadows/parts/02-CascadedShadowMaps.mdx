## 2. Cascaded shadow maps

Cascades start from a pretty simple observation: not every part of the screen needs the same amount of detail.

Right in front of the camera, a single shadow texel can cover dozens of pixels, so it'd better be tiny. A hundred metres
away, that same texel shrinks to a pixel or less, and nobody would notice if it was ten times bigger.

So instead of one map stretched over everything, the view gets cut into a few slices by distance, and each slice gets its own map.
The first one covers just the few metres in front of the camera, the next one a bit more, and the last one everything far away,
all at the same resolution but with a very different amount of ground per texel.

It's what the big public engines have been shipping for years. Unity calls them shadow cascades, Godot splits the view in four by
default, and Unreal still has them, even though virtual shadow maps have been the default for new projects since Unreal Engine 5.

Three.js ships one too, `CSMShadowNode`, and that's the one I tried back then. It's been more than a year though, and honestly I
don't remember much about how it behaved in Revo, besides never getting it to work well, so what follows comes more from research
than from my own experience.

I still wanted it in here, since cascades are the step between a single shadow map and virtual shadow maps, and the picture
wouldn't be complete without them. It's also why this chapter comes without screenshots: Revo already runs on virtual shadow maps,
and I didn't want to bend the project just to showcase cascades.

Picking the right cascade in the shader is the easy part: take the pixel's depth from the camera, find the slice it falls in,
and run the same comparison from the previous chapter against that slice's map.

The interesting part is deciding where to cut. Let's take as an example a camera that sees from half a metre up to 150 metres,
split into three cascades with a 2048 map each.

There are a few common ways to place the cuts:

- **Uniform** &rarr; slices of equal length, so the first one would run all the way to 50 metres and lose its detail.
- **Logarithmic** &rarr; each slice longer than the previous one by a fixed factor, so the first one would end at about 3 metres
  and the last one gets huge.
- **Practical** &rarr; halfway between the two, the Three.js default, which cuts at about 25 and 60 metres.

With the practical cuts, each slice gets a square map as wide as its longest diagonal, so the slice fits in it whichever way the
camera turns:

- **0.5 to 25 m** &rarr; a map of about 26 by 26 metres, roughly 1.3 centimetres per texel.
- **25 to 60 m** &rarr; about 41 by 41 metres, roughly 2 centimetres per texel.
- **60 to 150 m** &rarr; about 104 by 104 metres, around 5.1 centimetres per texel.

Compare that with a single 4096 map holding the whole 150 metres, which gives about 3.8 centimetres per texel everywhere. Three
2048 maps add up to only three quarters of its texels, so they take 48 MiB instead of 64, and yet the near slice is three times
sharper and the middle one twice as sharp, with only the far one a bit worse, where nobody notices.

Noice :)

The drawings keep the frustum narrow to fit the page, so there each slice's longest diagonal runs mostly along its length. A real
perspective camera usually spreads much wider, so each slice ends up wider than it is long, and then it's mostly the width that
decides how much ground its square has to cover.

The maps are also drawn from above, while the real ones are rendered from the sun and line up with its direction, and the ruler
starts at 0 instead of half a metre, just to keep the numbers round.

Either way the idea stays the same: each map is a square big enough to hold its whole slice.

### Following the camera

These maps now follow the camera around though, and that brings a few problems of its own.

The first one is shimmering, and it's easy to picture. Whenever the camera moves, the map moves with it and lands a fraction of a
texel away from where it was, so the edges get rasterized slightly differently every frame and shadows start crawling as you walk.
The fix is to move the map only in whole texel steps, so the texel grid stays put and the edges with it. Three.js does that by
snapping each cascade's centre to its texel size.

Turning the camera causes the same crawling in a different way. The slice turns with the camera, so a box fitted tightly around
it would change size every frame, and the texels with it. That's why each cascade is sized by the slice's longest diagonal, as in
the example above, and keeps that size whichever way the camera turns. A bit of resolution goes to waste in the corners, but it holds
still.

Then there are the seams, since the ground right at a cut suddenly switches to bigger texels, from about 2 to 5.1 centimetres at
60 metres in the example above, and that jump can show up as a line on the ground following the camera around, not exactly subtle.
`CSMShadowNode` has an optional `fade` that blends the two cascades across a small margin to hide it. That same jump matters for
the bias from the previous chapter too, since bigger texels need a bigger tolerance, so Three.js scales it up for each cascade.

The last one is easy to miss. The cascades are rendered from the sun's point of view, but each render still goes through an
orthographic camera, and a camera has to be placed somewhere. It only draws what sits between its near and far planes, so anything
standing further toward the sun than the camera itself never makes it into the map, even when its shadow falls right in view.

With a single shadow map, that place is the `DirectionalLight`'s own position: its shadow camera sits there, looks at the light's
target, and draws from 0.5 to 500 units ahead by default. Cascades place their own light cameras instead, one per slice, each
centred on its slice as the sun sees it and right at the slice's edge closest to the sun. A camera that close would miss any caster
just off screen, like a tall tree between the sun and the ground in view, so Three.js pulls every cascade's light camera toward the
sun by the same `lightMargin`, 200 units by default, which should be plenty for most scenes.

### Drawing it all again

Even when they hold still, cascades pay the same price as the single map, just several times over. Each cascade is its own render
from the sun, and every caster that overlaps it gets drawn into it, so a tree sitting right at a cut ends up in both of them. With
`autoUpdate` on by default, that happens every single frame, whether anything moved or not.

And in Revo, almost nothing that casts a shadow moves. The trees, the rocks, the statues and the campfire stay right where they
are, and the sun doesn't move unless I move it. Redrawing all of them every frame, sometimes into more than one cascade, just to get
the exact same depth back is a lot of work for nothing.

One trick is to refresh the far cascades less often, but the static stuff still gets redrawn from scratch, just not every frame.

What I actually wanted was the opposite: draw the static stuff once and keep it, redraw only what changed, and instead of deciding
where the detail goes by distance, let the screen ask for exactly the parts of the map it needs.

That's pretty much what virtual shadow maps are about.
