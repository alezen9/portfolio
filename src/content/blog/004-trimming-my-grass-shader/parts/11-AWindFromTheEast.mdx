## 11. A wind from the East

About two years ago I played Ghost of Tsushima for the first time.

I had already seen screenshots and clips from other people's playthroughs, and those were what first inspired the grass field. Playing
it myself was a completely different story though.

I could stop almost anywhere just to admire the scenery, which happened quite often because the game made almost everywhere worth
looking at.

Among everything that made me fall in love with it, the guiding wind is still one of my favourite features.

It simply looked beautiful! It was genuinely useful and it let the HUD stay remarkably clean.

Swipe upward and the world itself points towards your destination. No line painted on the ground and no marker asking to be
followed every second.

At the time of writing, figuratively speaking of course, I am playing Ghost of Yotei, which uses the same mechanism, but Tsushima
is where the idea came from for me.

Revo already had directional wind around the time I shared the previous article. The idea of making it visible with particles and
lines had been there since I first added it. I just kept postponing that part until now.

I do not want to disappear into every navigation system in the middle of a grass article. The particles and streaks are new, they
move together with the grass, and that makes them connected enough for this little detour. Revo is open source anyway, so the
radial menu, landmarks and everything around them are there for anyone curious enough to dig further.

<br />

### One wind to rule them all

Before the `WindManager`, trees, flowers and grass each had their own version of wind. They could all move, but there was no shared
directional event feeding them the same information.

The manager filled that gap. It runs on the CPU, owns the direction and intensity, then exposes them as TSL uniforms that shaders
across Revo can consume:

```typescript
export class WindManager {
  private _uDirection = uniform(new Vector2(0, -1));
  private _uIntensityDirectional = uniform(0);

  // ...

  get uDirection() {
    return this._uDirection;
  }

  get uIntensityDirectional() {
    return this._uIntensityDirectional;
  }
}
```

Selecting a discovered landmark gives it a target, and a trackpad swipe up starts four simple phases:

1. **Calculate direction** &rarr; Point from the player's current position towards the selected landmark.
2. **Ramp intensity** &rarr; Smoothly increase the directional wind until it reaches full strength.
3. **Hold** &rarr; Keep it there for 3 seconds.
4. **Decay** &rarr; Gradually bring the directional intensity back to zero.

The direction is calculated once when the gust begins. Moving during those few seconds does not rotate it halfway through. Once it
ends, another swipe calculates a fresh direction, while reaching the landmark clears the target automatically.

Grass, flowers, particles and streaks all receive that same direction and intensity. Each system still decides how much it bends,
how fast it moves and what full strength means for it.

> One might rightfully ask: "Why didn't you also mention trees?"
>
> Fair question. Sharp eyes. Their shadows are currently baked, and making the trees sway much more than a subtle oscillation would
> most likely break the illusion. Maybe in the not so distant future...who knows :)

### Volumetric air

The names moved around a little while I worked on this. They started as lines, the longer version became ribbons, and the shorter
trails I eventually kept are now streaks. Apparently even naming them needed a few passes :)

The current visual layer uses a `56` &times; `56` field of sprite particles, `3,136` in total, and `12` streaks.

I see the small particles as pollen or dust giving some volume to the air. The streaks are the readable part of the gust: long,
soft strokes making the direction obvious without adding an arrow to the screen.

The particles were the simpler half.

Their field covers `170` world units and stays centred around the player. I begin with a `56` &times; `56` grid, then give every
cell a stable random offset. The distribution remains fairly even without looking like graph paper.

Everything one particle needs fits inside one `vec4`: the first three components hold its XYZ position, while `w` stores its age.

The initial compute pass fills that buffer, then the update pass takes over. Nothing on the CPU creates or removes individual
particles.

Age decides when a particle gets another life. Each one receives a slightly different lifetime from its stable hash, and once that
time expires the compute shader reuses the same buffer slot. It places the particle on the upwind half of the field, anywhere across
its width, then moves its age slightly below zero to add a short delay before it becomes visible again.

Both the initial spawn and every later respawn sample the terrain height and grass mask at the new position. A particle over grass
receives that terrain height plus a small random offset. One outside it gets a height of `0`, which the material recognises as an
invalid spawn and keeps hidden until the next attempt.

I only sample the grass mask at that point. Once a valid particle starts travelling, the wind is free to carry it above sand or
water. Checking the mask continuously would make it pop in and out every time it crossed the edge of a grassy area.

The shared wind direction provides the forward movement. A sample from the noise atlas adds some sideways and vertical drift,
along with small changes in speed, so the whole field does not travel as one suspiciously organised block. The field follows the
player, wrapping particles around its edges as Revo moves.

The compute simulation continues between gusts, even while every particle is scaled down to zero. A stable hash gives each one a
slightly different point during the intensity ramp at which it starts growing into view. The same happens in reverse during the
decay, so they disappear gradually without another CPU-side fade or any transparency tricks.

Keeping this small simulation alive gives every gust something already in motion instead of a field visibly starting from rest. I
have not measured it as a problem worth complicating yet.

### A trail of positions

The visible lines took a few more attempts.

The first version gave every line one position and asked the material to build the whole body around it with sine curves and noise.
The result could move nicely, but a long shape knowing only the terrain height under its centre was always going to meet a hill
eventually.

I tried sampling the terrain for every vertex. That stopped the line from passing straight through the ground. The movement no
longer looked as smooth though, and I could suddenly see the individual segments as it curved.

The next experiment used a different representation, and for a little while I kept both versions around while deciding which one I
preferred. The first streaks were quite fat and very linear. Then they undulated too much. I kept adjusting their width, heading,
length and speed until the shorter trails looked much closer to what I had in mind, then removed the ribbons and kept those.

The current version is easier to picture as one moving point dragging a short memory of where it has been. That point is the head
of the streak.

In code, every streak is an instance of the same plane, split into `24` segments. Its shape comes from `25` positions stored in an
SSBO: one current head and the history needed by the remaining segments. Each point stores its XYZ position, with `w` currently
unused. A second, much smaller buffer stores one age per streak. That is all the per-streak state stored in the two buffers.

The initial compute pass places every streak somewhere inside the field and gives it a different starting age. From there the update
pass owns its lifecycle. A negative age keeps it waiting, reaching zero lets it move and reaching the end of its lifetime sends it
to a new position with another short delay. The CPU never needs to spawn a streak or move one around.

The head follows the direction from `WindManager`, although not perfectly. The noise atlas bends that direction slightly, a sine
adds a gentle swirl and a few stable variations change the speed. The field itself is centred a little ahead of the player along
that same wind direction.

As the head moves, a small CPU-side timer decides when its position should be recorded. Every `0.075` seconds the next slot in the
ring buffer becomes the new head. The compute shader copies the previous position into that slot before moving it, while
`uRecordPhase` describes how far the current interval has progressed.

The material then walks backwards through that ring buffer. Each row of the segmented plane reads an older pair of positions and
interpolates between them using `uRecordPhase`, which keeps the trail moving smoothly between recordings. Those neighbouring
positions also provide the direction of the trail. Crossing it with the camera direction gives the material the sideways axis it
needs to spread the vertices into a camera-facing strip.

This representation also made the terrain problem much easier. The compute shader first settles the head's final XZ position,
including wrapping or respawning, and only then samples the heightmap there. It gently moves the head towards that terrain height
plus its own lift, and the resulting Y position becomes part of the recorded history.

The material only reads those stored positions. It does not need to sample the terrain for every vertex anymore.

If a streak wraps around the field or respawns somewhere else, all `25` positions move together. Otherwise the head would appear on
one side while the tail stretched across the entire world trying to catch up. Different starting ages, lengths and speed pulses
keep all `12` from marching across the screen in formation.

I am glad I finally built this small piece before moving on to culling. I had wanted it in Revo since the first time I saw it done
so well in someone else's game I loved.
