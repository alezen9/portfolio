## Catching the wind

The July wind was a push. A force along the wind direction, the same for every point of the flag, plus some sine waves on top to make
it flutter. Seven hand-tuned numbers in total, and none of them meant anything physical.

It also behaves oddly if you think about it. A flag turned edge-on to the wind gets pushed exactly as hard as one facing it, and once
it's fully streaming there's no reason for it to keep waving, which is why the sine waves were there in the first place.

Real wind doesn't push cloth, it flows around it. NvCloth models each little piece of cloth as a small plate in a stream of air, and
the idea is easy to picture. A piece facing the wind catches all of it. A piece edge-on catches almost nothing. A tilted one gets
pushed sideways too, like a hand out of a car window.

{/* illustration F8: three small plates in the same horizontal wind stream (arrows from the left). 1: facing the wind, a big drag arrow. 2: edge-on, almost no arrow. 3: tilted, a drag arrow along the wind plus a lift arrow pointing sideways out of the plate. Labels "all of it", "almost nothing", "drag and lift". */}

For every point, in every step:

```text
u       = wind velocity - point velocity
facing  = normal . u / |u|
drag    = u * |facing| * |u| * dragCoefficient
lift    = (normal - direction of u * facing) * facing * |u|^2 * liftCoefficient
```

**Drag** pushes along the relative wind, scaled by how much the cloth faces it. **Lift** pushes across it. The normal is the same one
I was already computing for the lighting, so it came almost for free.

This is also where the waving comes from, without any sine waves: any small ripple tilts some parts of the cloth, which then catch the
wind differently, which makes new ripples. The existing wind noise now varies the wind speed across the flag instead of scaling a
push, and that's what gives it gusts.

<br />

The seven magic numbers became three knobs that actually mean something: wind speed, drag and lift. The drag value of 3 even lands
close to the physical one for a light fabric in air, which was a nice surprise.

It didn't cost anything measurable either, the whole simulation ended up at about 0.065 ms.

<br />

I'll be honest though: if you stand still in a steady wind and look at the flag, the difference with the old version is subtle. It
shows when the wind changes direction, when the flag turns relative to it, and in how the free edge curls and moves on its own
instead of following a pattern.

For me that was worth it. The knobs mean something now, and nothing about the motion is scripted anymore.
