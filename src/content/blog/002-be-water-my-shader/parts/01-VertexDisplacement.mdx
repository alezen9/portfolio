## 1. Vertex displacement approach

In my first attempt, I took the most straightforward path: ==vertex displacement==.
It's as simple as it sounds: move vertices up and down by some amount derived from sine waves, noise,
or a mix of both, add time as part of the input, and the surface starts to ripple.

This alone isn't enough to sell the effect, though. Even if vertices are moving, the surface can still look flat.

<br />

### Normal and fragment

To make the next step clear, let's first familiarize ourselves with two concepts: ==normal== and ==fragment==.

<br />

**What is a normal?**

Normals are 3D unit vectors that help the renderer determine which way a surface is facing, crucial for
lighting and reflections.  
They're stored **per vertex** and then interpolated across the surface of each triangle so every fragment
gets an approximate orientation.

When a shape has a hard edge, normals are discontinuous, which means the vertex at that corner must
be duplicated so each copy can carry its own normal.

A cube is the classic example: although it has 8 corners, in practice we use 24 vertices (4 per face)
so that each face keeps its own normal. Otherwise, with only 8 shared vertices, the normals would be averaged
and the cube would look oddly rounded.

**What is a fragment?**

Internally, all meshes are broken down into tiny triangles (primitives).  
When one of those triangles overlaps a pixel on screen, the rasterizer produces a **fragment**:
a candidate contribution to that pixel's final color.

If several primitives overlap the same pixel, multiple fragments are generated in the same frame.
Each runs through the ==fragment shader== (called a _pixel shader_ in DirectX), then passes a set of per-fragment tests (depth, stencil, alpha).  
Eventually, some are kept and blended, others are discarded.

That means a single pixel can be touched by multiple fragments, like in the example below:

<br />

Here, the same pixel is covered by the sky, then the ground, then the box, and finally the sphere.  
Each overlap generates a fragment, but only the front-most opaque one wins.  
If blending is involved (e.g. additive blending), several fragments may combine instead of being discarded.

This is why OpenGL calls it a _fragment shader_: it doesn't shade "pixels" directly, but every fragment
that might affect them.

> Shading fragments that never contribute to the final image is called **overdraw**. GPUs try to minimize
> it with early depth testing, front-to-back rendering order, or a depth pre-pass
> (rendering depth first, then shading only visible surfaces).  
> However, these optimizations often don't apply when transparency or blending is involved, since
> even covered fragments may still affect the pixel color.

### Shading waves

Back to our waves!  
At this point each vertex is moving up and down in a wave-like pattern but the surface still looks
flat, next step is shading it.

On a plain surface, all vertex normals usually point upward. We need a way to vary them so light bounces
differently at each fragment. A common trick for displaced geometry is the ==neighbor technique==:

1. Compute elevation for two nearby theoretical points, slightly offset from the current vertex on X and Z by a tiny `ε`.
2. Compute elevation for current vertex.
3. Build two direction vectors from the current elevated position to the two neighbors.
4. Take the cross product and normalize to approximate the normal.

<br />

```glsl
float getElevation(vec3 position) {
  // ... sample noise / texture / function
  return elevation;
}

const EPSILON = 0.01; // tune to your scale (world units)

void main() {
  // ...
  vec3 xNeighborPosition = currentVertexPosition.xyz + vec3(EPSILON, 0.0, 0.0);
  xNeighborPosition.y += getElevation(xNeighborPosition);

  vec3 zNeighborPosition = currentVertexPosition.xyz + vec3(0.0, 0.0, EPSILON);
  zNeighborPosition.y += getElevation(zNeighborPosition);

  currentVertexPosition.y += getElevation(currentVertexPosition.xyz);

  // order matters (right-hand rule), pick one and stay consistent
  vec3 toXNeighbor = normalize(xNeighborPosition - currentVertexPosition.xyz);
  vec3 toZNeighbor = normalize(zNeighborPosition - currentVertexPosition.xyz);
  vec3 computedNormal = normalize(cross(toXNeighbor, toZNeighbor));
}
```

> Beware that `ε` is a balancing act. Too large and you'll smear away fine detail, too small and
> floating-point noise/aliasing creeps in. Each neighbor adds a texture fetch, so the cost scales with vertex count.

<br />

During my research, at the time, I stumbled on a great [YouTube demo by Dan Greenheck](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jK4uXGY07vA),
where he builds a water shader in `WebGL` using this technique.

His version looks fantastic, but it's also quite heavy. My take was a trimmed-down version:
I cut some steps, didn't accumulate noise octaves, and sampled from a precomputed noise texture instead
of calculating noise procedurally.

That noise texture actually packed three different tiled noises (Perlin, Voronoi, Turbulence) into its RGB channels.
I mixed between them instead of accumulating octaves. This cut computation, but at the cost of depth and richness,
Dan's multi-octave approach had much more variety.

Most likely the biggest bottleneck in my version was texture sampling.  
Every vertex had to do three fetches just to get displacement data: one for its own elevation and two more for its neighbors.
On a dense mesh, that scales up quickly. Combined with my shortcuts, the result had motion, but the surface
looked flatter and more repetitive than I hoped.

To make the most of this approach in my case, I also built a custom lake mesh in Blender. Displacement only moves
existing vertices, so I gave the surface plenty of subdivisions while cutting out everything hidden under the terrain.  
The result was an irregular, lake-shaped mesh with triangles only where they mattered.
